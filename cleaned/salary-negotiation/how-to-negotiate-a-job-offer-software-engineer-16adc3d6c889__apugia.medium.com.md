You might be looking for some general advice on how to navigate your first job offer, or maybe you switched careers and you're not sure what the conversation and/or negotiation should look like for a Software Engineering job. That's okay, a lot of people aren't really sure how or where to begin. But, before we get started…. *Congratulations!* You just interviewed your butt off and you should be proud of all the things you've accomplished.

**P.S.** If you already have a contract/offer letter in-hand but aren't sure how to proceed, I recommend skipping to the second part as it's likely more relevant to your current situation.

### Part 1: How to Navigate an Upcoming Job Offer

> "…all of my interviews were scheduled informally/directly, i.e. without any kind of job description or written information anywhere, so everything I know is based on assumptions from zoom conversations. I was assuming based on everything that it would be salary, but now I am wondering, since it's a startup, if that's even possible… I feel like I'm going into a negotiation call with no idea what to expect."

Someone reached out to one of the coding communities I follow with this message and was confused/stressed/overwhelmed. This is totally normal when navigating a job offer. It's complicated nowadays, especially when you're broke and desperate for money, and to top it off, maybe you're too afraid to negotiate a counter offer because you're worried they might rescind their original offer. But, I'll tell you the same thing I told the person who asked for help: **You are worth a lot more than you know.**

So with this in mind, the time has come… your interviewer, whoever they may be, has either just given you a verbal offer over the phone or said that they were about to email you the contract/offer letter. You're going to do three things:

1. Listen to what they say on the phone carefully and take notes if you can. They might clarify things in the contract/offer letter that could be confusing or hard to understand.- Be *very* nice and cordial, **whether or not** you're happy with their offer. They will probably tell you your hourly or salary rate, explain benefits and bonuses, and tell you how much time off you will accrue and how many of those hours carry over into the next year. Always say that you are very excited and appreciative.- **Do not accept the offer, no matter how great it is.** I know this might seem strange, but you have to review everything they just said and also see all the legal jargon (aka. non-compete information/duration, time-off requests, dress code, legal job title, etc.) If they do not give or email you the contract/offer letter, ask them to email you a copy.

For example, after they extend the offer, I always say something along the lines of, "Thank you so much for the offer, I appreciate it so much! Is there any way you can send the contract/offer letter to my email so that I can read it over and review everything you just said? I do better when I read things."

Any reputable company will be completely understanding and agree to this. They may give you a deadline for when they want a response, or they may not.. This is normal. For the most part, you're in control now.

### Part 2: What to Do With an Offer Letter or Contract

1. After you get the contract in-hand (or, in-email), read it 10 or more times over. I'm not exaggerating. Seriously, read everything at least 3 times before taking any notes or asking any questions.- Print it out, make a copy, or save it as a PDF and take notes directly on the document. Write down things that you have questions about or don't agree with. This is handy when asking about benefits, time off, non-competes, dress code, work from home possibilities, etc.- If you want something that's not on the contract/offer letter at all, write it in, within reason of course. Don't expect to get an additional $2,000/mo to cover your expensive gym membership and nutritional supplements, although that *would* be cool.- Check your job title on the document and google what your city's average industry standard salary is for that specific job title. (If your contract/offer letter says Software Engineer, don't look up Software Developer.)
         For example: You live in NYC and have just been offered a position as a Jr. Software Engineer.
         As of today, the average salary for a Jr. Software Engineer is $74,644/yr with an average annual cash bonus of $7,446.
         The average for a Jr. Software *Developer* is only $67,001/yr.- If they are offering you a salary *below* 5k what your city's average is, you need to ask for 5k ABOVE **your city's** average. This gives you a little bit of wiggle room for negotiations and also lets them know that you know what's up. Maybe they're just trying to take advantage of your inexperience and low-ball you.
           **Worst case scenario: don't accept less than the lowest range for your city, ever.** If your city's standard salary is between 65k-85k, never accept less than 65k.- If they are a startup and **only offering you equity** instead of pay, keep in mind that this is a HUGE red flag and you should likely turn the offer down. For the most part, minimum wage laws require that you are paid, regardless of whether or not you are getting equity. Equity is cool, equity is fine, but equity doesn't replace money. Equity is a bonus just like paid time off, healthcare, and bonuses.- After you understand everything in the contract/offer letter and have sufficiently marked it up with questions/comments/counter offers, contact your interviewer and schedule a time to go over everything with them either in-person, over the phone, or over zoom. Ask all of your questions in the order that they appear on the contract/offer letter (top to bottom) but leave the salary discussion for last.- Don't be afraid to play a little bit of hard ball. The worst they can say is, "No sorry, we can't do \_\_\_\_\_\_" and offer an alternative.- Last but certainly not least, don't be a bad or rude negotiator. Now is a good time to really listen to what they're saying and make a call based on what they're saying. You might be able to negotiate them for $500 more per year on top of what you've already gotten, but is it really worth it? That's for you to decide. Don't be too pushy. You're worth a lot but you're not worth a headache.
