Six years ago, I went from being a Senior Engineer to an Engineering Manager almost overnight. I thought it would be a smooth transition — after all, I was already mentoring, leading projects, and writing design docs.

**I was wrong.**

My first 90 days as an EM were a whirlwind of confusing priorities, awkward conversations, and unexpected blind spots. I quickly realized that technical excellence didn't automatically translate to leadership success.

This is the first article in a series where I'll share real stories from my journey — mistakes I made, lessons I learned, and practical tools that actually helped me grow as a leader.

If you're about to step into management (or already there and feeling stuck), this might save you some headaches — or at least make you feel less alone.

Let's start with the **three biggest lessons** I learned in those chaotic first 90 days. They became the core advice I give to every new engineering manager I mentor.

As my previous leader once told me:
> *📝R "Choose your battles wisely. Sometimes it's fine to lose one battle to win the important one."*

### *🚩 Lesson 1: You Don't Have to Solve Everything Yourself*

*When I first became a manager, I felt pressure to have all the answers — whether about technical decisions, team tensions, or product priorities. I thought being the "go-to" person meant I was doing my job.*

***In reality?** I became my team's biggest bottleneck — and I burned out quickly.
Approvals piled up, decisions stalled, and I was constantly stuck firefighting.*

*A game-changing moment came during a casual 1:1 with our CTO. I asked how they handled these situations, and they said:*
> *"George, if you want to be successful, count how often your team needs you while you're on vacation.*

> *Do you want to be the hero — or build a team that doesn't need a hero?"*

*That completely changed my mindset.*

#### *✅ The Fix: Start Delegating Early*

*Here's what worked for me:*

* *Delegating ownership of agile ceremonies like stand-ups and retrospectives (we'll revisit this in future blog posts).** *Letting the team run tech talks — I just joined as a participant.** *Asking team members to propose solutions before I weighed in.*

*It was uncomfortable at first, but within months, the team moved faster, was more engaged, and made smarter decisions — without me in every loop. Now, after 6 years i still remember the first learning and also learned to show the way to other but not telling the how!(we'll also talk about it later).*
> ****Key takeaway:*** *Your job isn't to solve everything — it's to create an environment where your team solves things confidently and independently.**

### *🚩 Lesson 2: Managing Up Is as Important as Managing Down*

*Early on, I made the classic mistake of focusing entirely on my team and ignoring upwards communication. I assumed leadership would just "get it."*

*Spoiler: they don't.*

*Managing up means **proactively sharing your team's wins, challenges, and needs with your manager and stakeholders**. When you do it right, you earn trust, avoid misalignment, and unlock resources faster.*

*I learned this the hard way while trying to tackle too many strategic projects. During a 1:1, my manager asked me to list everything my team was handling — and suddenly realized we were overloaded. Shortly after, they unlocked headcount to help us succeed.*

#### *✅ The Fix: Communicate Early, Before Escalations*

* *Regular updates on progress and challenges — not just problems.** *Flag risks early so leadership isn't caught by surprise.** *Share team wins to build confidence in your leadership.*

> ****Key takeaway:*** *Visibility upwards isn't self-promotion — it's how you secure the right support for your team.**

### *🚩 Lesson 3: Redefining Success — It's No Longer About You*

*As an engineer, success was simple: write code, ship features, get feedback.
As a manager, I struggled because **my individual performance stopped being the measure of success**.*

*Success became about:*

* ***Team growth**** ***Morale and retention**** ***Team's collective delivery and impact***

*It was uncomfortable at first — especially when the wins were less visible. But over time, I learned to celebrate team successes and step back so others could shine.*

*One of the most impactful things I did was **set success metrics with my direct manager—**focused on **team outcomes,** notpersonal output**. (We'll deep-dive into this topic later.** Let's also learn more about **managing your manager.)***
> ****Key takeaway:*** *Your success as a manager is measured by how your team performs, grows, and thrives — not by how many problems you personally solve.**
