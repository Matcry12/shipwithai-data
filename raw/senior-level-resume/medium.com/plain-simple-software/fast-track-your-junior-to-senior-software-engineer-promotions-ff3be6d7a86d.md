Most people spend two to three years as a junior software engineer. Then they spend another three to five years as a software engineer before finally being promoted to a senior software engineer. I went from junior software engineer to senior software engineer in two years. How? Through an understanding of what's expected at each level, finding a way to get experience outside of my job, and switching companies.

### Software Engineering Levels and Expectations

Understanding which responsibilities and abilities separate the different levels of software engineers is a good place to start. Obviously, experience separates these roles, but experience with what? The difference between Junior Software Engineers and [associate] Software Engineers is mainly skill-based. However, to become a Senior Software Engineer, you not only have to have the software engineering skillset but also understand more business-level topics.

#### Junior Software Engineer

Junior Software Engineers are not expected to know too much. Most people at this level are recruited straight out of college or have only been in the industry for a short time. Most of your responsibilities at this level are going to be building smaller projects, maintenance, and adding small features to existing software. If you're lucky like me, you'll get put on a big project after your first couple of successes and that will help boost your career.

The most important things to learn as a Junior Software Engineer are abstractions. You should become familiar with software engineering patterns like the [Orchestrator Pattern](https://pythonalgos.com/a-software-engineers-guide-to-the-orchestrator-pattern/), learn about [Serverless vs Server Architectures](https://pythonalgos.com/software-architectures-serverless-and-server-traditional/), and software development principles like the single responsibility principle. You should also become familiar with things like interfaces and understand [what an API is](https://pythonalgos.com/what-is-an-api-and-how-do-i-use-it/) and how to use and make one.

Expect to spend a lot of time reading code, looking around existing repositories, and updating some outdated common packages. You should take advantage of this time to be learning about as many packages as possible. Not for the contents, but to understand the structures. You should also reach out to more senior members of your team for help when you can. Don't spend too much time struggling on problems alone.

#### Software Engineer

At this intermediate level, you should understand how to do system design. Software Engineers are expected to be able to build components independently. At this level, you should have a basic mastery of most abstractions and design patterns. You should be able to understand and generate both [high-level design documents](https://pythonalgos.com/how-to-create-a-high-level-design-document/) and [low-level design documents](https://pythonalgos.com/how-to-create-a-low-level-design-document/).

At big tech companies (like FAANG companies), one of the core components of promotions is your ability to articulate the value of your work. If you're a strong Junior Software Engineer and you have a good manager, you will learn about this from your manager. You should have a couple of contributions to business-critical projects and created a few million dollars of value. By that point, you should easily be able to argue for a promotion.

To get promoted to a Senior Software Engineer, your main goal is to learn how to tie your contributions to business-level goals and build more complex systems. Questions you should ask include questions like:

* "How does this affect business goals?"* "Which of these are the most relevant to our organization's business goals?"* "Why are we designing *x* feature?"

#### Senior Software Engineer

Senior Software Engineers have a lot of responsibility. You're not just responsible for designing projects, you're also responsible for driving business-level goals. At this level, you should be able to answer all the questions above that I suggested Software Engineers to be asking. You should have an in-depth familiarity with the software patterns and principles, and you should be seeking new ones to learn.

Senior Software Engineers also spend a lot of time helping more junior members out. You may be spending over 50% of your time doing this. At this level, it's hard to find more senior members to reach out to because there are only two levels about Senior Software Engineers at most companies, and they're rare to find. Most people don't ever make it to this level by the way. It's not uncommon for people to never rise above a regular Software Engineer. This is because of the amount of work required to become a Senior Software Engineer.

At this level the questions you should be asking and trying to answer are:

* "How can we most effectively create features that will expand our business-level goals?"* "How do we allocate resources to best meet or exceed development goals?"* "Which technologies should we be adopting?"* "Why should we use *x* technology for this instead of *y* technology?"* "How will this particular project advance the business?"

### Getting the Experience

Now that we've taken a look at the difference between the different roles, let's take a look at how you can get the experience. Other than work, your best bet to move from Junior Software Engineer to Software Engineer is to do personal projects. Doing many personal projects will give you the ability to elevate your programming and design abilities.

To get experience on the business side and become an effective Senior Software Engineer, you'll need to get some exposure to the business. You can do this by asking the right questions at work, or by starting some side business of your own. Your ability to earn money from external jobs probably has to go through some sort of evaluation from your company. Most big tech firms will allow you to do some work outside of the company as long as it doesn't interfere with your actual work. Be aware, that they may claim a portion of the project's profits if you manage to get one.

The best way to get this business experience is to find a way to turn one or more of your personal projects into something you can sell. You can also try e-commerce or something of that nature, but that doesn't have enough to do with software engineering to really get you much experience. The best bet here is to create a SaaS software or a web API and market that. You may not get actual profit but generating some revenue and getting experience in the sales and marketing side of the business will help you articulate why features should be built and how to plan feature roadmaps.

#### Personal Projects

Let's first address personal projects. As a Junior Software Engineer, you won't be given many big projects to do until you show you can handle them. There are multiple ways to get to that level, but an easy way to do that is by having larger personal projects.

Most Junior Software Engineers don't have too many projects under their belt yet. Depending on your level, you'll want to start off with smaller projects that you can easily handle. Once you get the confidence that you can build things, you'll want to move on to an area of interest.

For example, if you're interested in Machine Learning you'll want to start off with something simple like a [Linear Regression](https://pythonalgos.com/introduction-to-machine-learning-linear-regression/). Then, you'll want to move on to something more difficult like [building your own neural network](https://pythonalgos.com/create-a-neural-network-from-scratch-in-python-3/). The next step from there would be to learn how to scale a machine learning infrastructure and apply it. Examples of applied machine learning projects could be [digit recognition](https://pythonalgos.com/the-best-rnn-for-image-classification-rnn-lstm-or-gru/) or [text processing](https://pythonalgos.com/using-nlp-to-get-insights-from-twitter/).

It's important to remember that you'll encounter a lot of difficulties along the way. You'll feel like an imposter quite a bit when you can't figure out a challenge. Ultimately though, by driving your improvement yourself, you'll feel a sense of satisfaction and speed up your career.

#### Business and Leadership Experience

Getting programming experience is the easy part. That's what most software engineers like doing anyway. Getting the business and leadership experience necessary to understand how software impacts business is the hard part. As I said above, one of the ways to do this is to start your own business on the side. It doesn't have to be a huge tech startup or even turn a profit, the important thing is to learn.

Having a side business allows you to learn how software ties into business. It allows you to articulate why you made decision *x* in creating your software instead of decision *y*. For example, why did you build a backend using server instances instead of using a dedicated server? Was it because you only needed to ping the server every so often? Or was it because it was the quickest way to production? You'll have to think about and answer questions like these when you're in the position of a Senior Software Engineer. As with many things in life, you have to have the experience before you can get the position.

Leadership experience is even harder to develop. I've held leadership roles since high school, but not everyone has that fortune. One of the ways you can develop your leadership skills is by getting people to work on projects with you. Say you want to build a large project, find a friend or two and divvy up responsibilities. You can all learn, and you can all improve.

### Switching Companies

This is the real secret sauce to fast promotions. You can stay at a big tech company for years and not get promotions due to organizational changes and other factors of luck. The real reason doing outside projects can really fast track your promotions is not because you can show off those skills to your manager, it's so you can highlight them to other companies.

In times past, many people spent most of their professional career working at one company. Now, it's not uncommon to see Millennials switch between companies every few years. New estimates show that Gen-Z and Millennials may hold up to 14 different jobs in their professional career. The best software engineers typically hop between companies every two to three years. Switching companies may seem difficult, but beyond getting your first software engineering job, it's not hard at all.

Between my first and second job, I took a while off to build my own personal projects full time. That choice accelerated my career by allowing me to articulate learn how to build larger projects, articulate business interests, and run a team. Assuming you're a good software engineer (since you're reading this article, I assume you are), you should have your technical interview skills down pat.

The most important thing for you is to be able to articulate your projects. Be friendly in interviews, and don't be afraid to interview with multiple companies at a time. Even though I only got one offer from over 200 applications for my first job, I got 4 offers out of 5 interviews my second time job seeking. Good software engineers are hard to find, the market is in your favor.

### Summary of Fast Tracking Your Junior to Senior Software Engineer Promotions

The tricks to fast-tracking your promotion are to do outside projects, learn how software integrates with business, and jumping companies. Doing outside projects helps you build your technical skills and possibly your teamwork skills as well. It may even help you learn how software and business integrate and interact, an important [quality of good senior software engineers](https://blog.devgenius.io/the-qualities-of-a-good-senior-engineer-a3b5d1fe4fea). Finally, you should switch companies for faster promotions.
