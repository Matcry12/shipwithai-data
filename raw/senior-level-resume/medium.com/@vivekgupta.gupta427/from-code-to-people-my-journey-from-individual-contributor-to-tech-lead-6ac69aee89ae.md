Three years ago, I was promoted from Module Lead Software Engineer to Lead Software Engineer at a data analytics company. I thought I was ready. After all, I had been writing clean code, solving complex technical problems, and mentoring junior developers. How hard could leadership be?

I was about to find out.

**## The Wake-Up Call**

My first week as a tech lead, I was still deep in the codebase, reviewing every pull request in detail, rewriting code that didn't meet my standards, and staying late to fix issues myself. I thought this was what leadership looked like — being the best technical contributor on the team.

It gradually dawned on me that while I was buried in code, my team was stuck waiting for architectural decisions, blocked on unclear requirements, and struggling with priorities I hadn't communicated. They needed strategic direction and clarity, not another pair of hands writing code.

I realized I had been promoted for my technical skills, but the role required something entirely different: the ability to multiply the effectiveness of others, not just my own output.

**## The Mindset Shift: From "How" to "What" and "Why"**

The biggest change wasn't learning new frameworks or tools — it was shifting from thinking about *\*how\** to implement something to focusing on *\*what\** we should build and *\*why\** it matters.

**### Before: The Individual Contributor Mindset**

- "Let me write this critical component myself to ensure quality"

- "I need to review every line of code"

- "If I work harder, the project will succeed"

**### After: The Leadership Mindset**

- "How can I enable my team to deliver this with confidence?"

- "What context does my team need to make good decisions?"

- "How do I remove blockers so others can do their best work?"

This shift took months to internalize. I had to resist the urge to jump into code and instead ask: "Who on my team could own this? What do they need from me to succeed?"

**## The Hard Lessons I Learned**

**### 1. Technical Excellence ≠ Team Excellence**

Just because I could architect a perfect system didn't mean I could help five other engineers do the same. I learned that my job was to:

- Set clear technical standards and communicate the "why" behind them

- Create processes that helped the team maintain quality without my constant oversight

- Trust others to implement solutions, even if they weren't exactly how I would do it

**### 2. Communication Became My Most Important Skill**

As an IC, I could get away with being heads-down and communicating mainly through code. As a lead, I discovered that unclear communication was the root of most project failures.

I started:

- Writing detailed technical specs before any major work began

- Having regular 1:1s focused on career growth, not just project status

- Translating business requirements into technical terms and vice versa

- Over-communicating decisions and context to the team

**### 3. People Problems Are Harder Than Technical Problems**

Debugging a distributed system is complex, but it's predictable. People are not.

I faced situations I never encountered as an IC:

- A senior engineer who resisted code reviews

- Team members with conflicting technical opinions

- Burnout and motivation issues

- Balancing individual growth with project deadlines

I learned that solving people problems required empathy, patience, and sometimes difficult conversations — skills that no algorithm could teach me.

**## What Actually Worked**

**### 1. Servant Leadership in Practice**

Instead of being the smartest person in the room, I focused on being the most helpful:

- **\*\*Removing blockers\*\***: Spending time in meetings so my team could focus on coding

- **\*\*Providing context\*\***: Sharing the business reasoning behind technical decisions

- **\*\*Enabling growth\*\***: Pairing junior developers with challenging tasks and senior mentorship

**### 2. Building Systems, Not Dependencies**

I realized that if the team couldn't function without me, I wasn't leading — I was creating a bottleneck.

I started building systems:

- **\*\*Documentation\*\***: Comprehensive guides for our architecture and processes

- **\*\*Code review standards\*\***: Clear criteria so anyone could review effectively

- **\*\*Decision frameworks\*\***: Guidelines for technical choices when I wasn't available

**### 3. Measuring Success Differently**

My success metrics changed completely:

- **\*\*Before\*\***: Lines of code written, bugs fixed, features delivered by me

- **\*\*After\*\***: Team velocity, code quality across all contributors, team member growth, reduced escalations

**## The Ongoing Journey**

Three years later, I'm now preparing for the next step: Senior Lead Software Engineer. The challenges are evolving again:

- Leading multiple teams instead of one

- Influencing technical strategy across the organization

- Mentoring other tech leads

- Balancing technical depth with broader business impact

**## Key Takeaways for Aspiring Tech Leads**

1. **\*\*Your code is no longer your primary contribution\*\*** — your team's collective output is

2. **\*\*Invest heavily in communication skills\*\*** — they're as important as your technical abilities

3. **\*\*Learn to delegate effectively\*\*** — trust your team and give them room to grow

4. **\*\*Focus on systems and processes\*\*** — they scale better than individual heroics

5. **\*\*Embrace the discomfort\*\*** — leadership skills develop through challenging situations

**## The Reward**

Despite the challenges, leading a team has been incredibly rewarding. There's something powerful about watching a junior developer you mentored deliver a complex feature independently, or seeing your team rally together to solve a critical production issue.

The code I write today might be refactored next year, but the engineers I help develop will have careers that span decades.
