This month marks 2 years since my dream came true and my professional journey as a software developer started. And although sometimes it still feels surreal, at times it also can be overwhelming (no wonder I used the word "overwhelming" 9 times in this article), which can make people doubt if they're actually cut out for this job and if the career transition choice was a good idea at all.

Since I requalified as a dev after my academic career, I've worked in two different companies and regularly take notes of things I could do better if I had to re-do all this again. **I hope the practical advice in this article will make the transition as smooth as possible especially for people who, like me, went down the less traditional route of entering the software development field.**

#### ***This article is suitable for:***

* people who just got offered a job or are just starting their first job as a software developer* people who are applying for their first job and want to know what to expect (might be helpful for interviews to show you are prepared for the real world)* self-taught developers without a formal CS education, although some tips might be useful also for those who went down the traditional route

> ***Exclaimer***: Each company and team is different. I have worked in very small tech teams in non-tech companies — things are likely different in bigger teams and in tech-centred companies. So take everything with a grain of salt, of course.

#### Tip#1 : Software engineering is not just about writing code

Writing code is the tip of the iceberg in the software development lifecycle. When you join a company, you will be exposed to the entire software development lifecycle and all processes surrounding it:

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*HLh-leKPdXPLYjCRLhe8Fw.png)

For people without a CS education who had been writing scripts or working on projects solo, this might be a bit overwhelming at first.
> At your first job, be prepared to be unfamiliar with a lot of non-code related concepts - you're up for a journey to learn much more than writing better code .

#### Tip #2: Familiarise yourself with relevant tools before starting the job

I spent a lot of time doing countless tutorials on things I never needed. When you don't practise something, you forget it. In my next job, I then had do re-learn things basically from scratch.

Before starting the job, or literally on your first day, **I would ask the team lead or manager to give you a list of the tools they use.** For example:

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*AoTIPVh_Cw1MGVJvUEzNIw.png)

This list isn't compulsory or exhaustive, **your new manager would be in the best position to advise what you need to know** the first couple of months after you join. Personally, I would start reading around/practising *before* I start the job, but you may feel differently about it.

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*hBnMPQZ59PI0cXMBW3w-7w.png)

> ***Summary***: Targeted preparation. Becoming familiar with key tools used in your team isn't about becoming **an expert in any of this by your first day, week or even month,** **it's about gaining some familiarity, confidence, peace of mind, so you reduce level of overwhelm** you experience the first few weeks.

#### Tip #3: Stick to what the team knows

You *can* choose to use a different IDE or version control tool, but at the start, I would stick to what most of the team does, so they can easily help you when needed. People are busy and less keen to spend time helping you if it involves fiddling with a new tool they aren't familiar with.
> When you have gained confidence and independence, you can switch to your preferred software dev tools — until then, stick to what others use.

#### Tip #4: Expect to drown in an existing code base

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*UKkVPi42aXpApGtxNhZnzQ.png)

When you start your first job, you are unlikely to be given to create a project from scratch. I think one of the most overwhelming things a new developer might experience is the first month they are given access to the massive existing code base and asked to work on some small feature or bug (which is supposed to be the easy introduction into the project). You will see working on someone else's code can be scary and it takes a while to become comfortable with.
> ***Resource suggestion:***There are plenty of resources online that give excellent advice on how to approach a large code base (one example I like is this [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FErIfEd3IHI) by Tim Corey).

> ***Tip****:* Sometimes your best resource is your team. A helpful team-mate can give you valuable guidance, which can go a long way! Don't be afraid to ask which file would makes sense to start from.

#### Tip #5: Version control and CI/CD (DevOps)

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*zwdW5MyoRrqU10LkN8wW-g.png)

Tip #7 covers key concepts to be aware of, but I feel this one deserves its own section because it's something you'd be doing every day yet can get you in awkward situations.

* Don't start a job without being familiar and comfortable at least with using **git** on your own — ***adding, committing and pushing*** code from your local machine to a remote repository (e.g. GitHub).* Keep in mind that using version control in a **dev team** is a whole different thing and requires some time to get used to. Make sure you understand **local and remote repos, branches, what happens when you switch branches, what a pull request is, merging branches, pulling recent changes,** etc — again there are plenty of great resources online. To get started, here is a [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TPyfM8ns_MY) that I like.* Finally, get familiar with what happens after your code has made it to the remote repo — **what testing, deployment and release processes** are in place. This is part of what makes up the so-called **CI/CD pipeline** — also called DevOps CI/CD, if you want to be fancy.

> ***Resource suggestion:*** I find [this](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dl-BdxNRUqs) (part 1) and [this](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oNmcX6Gozg0) (part 2) videos actually very good at explaining the CI/CD process without going into **too much** specifics.

> ***Learning tip:***The less you know about something, the more generic the learning resources should be — think high level overview. If too specific, you will focus on certain technologies and processes that might not have much to do with what you do in your team.

#### Tip #6: Your job — the best time to learn !

**Instead of being overwhelmed, view the job as an opportunity to learn, to use your natural curiosity and interest in software engineering to develop a little bit every day.**

Your job is an opportunity to solidify some shaky knowledge you already have. A lot of the concepts you encounter, like many OOP principles, won't make sense until you actually start working on real enterprise code. So it's really a great time to learn, you should be excited about that!

During your job, you will be exposed to conversations even during daily stand up/Scrum where you won't understand anything. At this stage, no one expects you to understand everything and this is actually great! Instead of feeling overwhelmed, write down things you aren't familiar with and look them up later.

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*QdHZfLHEF3Y0n6ktb8G0JA.png)

For example you hear the team discussing GitHub Actions, workflows and release pipelines — go find a short [video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mFFXuXjVgkU) to get an overview what this is about (again nothing too detailed). I promise from the next day, things will make much more sense. Or you hear there is some problem with caching —write down *caching* and later go and read about how caching works in general, and then for your particular framework you use.
> ***Summary:*** **Do not ignore all the foreign words you hear at work —** chances are they are important concepts and they'll keep cropping up in future discussions, so make sure you at least know what they are next time they are mentioned. Once you do this systematically, things can only get easier from there.

People quickly takes things for granted after a couple of years in the field and forget everything they had to learn when they just started. As a junior, it's totally okay to ask a mid-level dev to spare 20–30 mins to talk you through the deployment process used in this team, the architecture or just a tip where to start from.

#### Tip #7: Some tech concepts to be familiar with

Having said that, there is some almost pre-assumed knowledge you need to have before starting a dev role (aside from using version control and CI/CD— see tip #5).

Depending on your prior experience, you might need to check these out before starting the job :

* environments — local, remote, development, production (names vary depending on the team) - make sure you understand what these are for and how you get from one to the other* client, server, client-server communication* API, RESTful API* request/response cycle, HTTP* data model* dependency* database fundamentals

> In the comments, feel free to suggest other absolutely necessary concepts you think anyone starting a job in SE should be aware of.

#### Tip #8: When you feel lost with a task, separate the noise from the info actually needed to solve that task…

Completing tasks as a junior when there is so much you don't know, particularly if it's in a new language or framework, can be overwhelming. Because of my background, I used to adopt a very academic approach — reading literally everything I don't know anything about, on the job. This meant that my productivity and sense of self-worth as a developer suffered.

My overwhelmed thought process went somehow like this:

*Ok I need to understand the current code to do my task. I see some lambda operator, open the documentation that leads me to .. lambda / anonymous functions. Then it goes into delegates. Hmm, delegates, I've heard that before, in that C# course I did last month, ok. But wait, what is Action<> and what is Func<>, is this also a delegate? …. \*tick tock tick tock \*… 2 hours later I am like — Oh no, I was supposed to be working on this task but instead I have no task and I am overwhelmed with all these concepts I am not sure I even understand. \*sigh\**

This lead to me feeling guilty for not progressing with the task and also that I don't know anything. You can't know everything, no one does. And it does get easier.

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*EqgmQDI2jMwv7CoLTrpYnA.png)

> ***Summary*****:** While working on your task, try to read the absolute minimum needed to understand what you're doing/what has been done and what you need to do to solve it. Dig as deep as you need to finish it (warning: it could be very deep depending on the problem you have). … Everything else, you consider as "**noise**". (see tip#9 for continuation)

#### … But keep track of what you need to learn (tip #9)

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*QMhl18MRydXeVSmiF4zYJQ.png)

But **you want to know about this "noise" later on too,** so you can become a good programmer — **don't just ignore concepts you aren't familiar with** just because you "got it to work" eventually — they will come back to bite you sooner or later.
So in parallel to working on the task, write down the "noise" on a piece of paper. As a very newbie C# developer, my first C# reading list looked something like this:

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*aO9VQFSHF1su02PECw2m7A.png)

(yes I actually found an old sticky note in one of my notebooks).

Then I spend time in the weekend or after work reading around each of these.
> ***Summary***: Read what is needed to get the job done, but take steps to steadily fill the gaps in your knowledge. Write them down on the side and catch up on them later, after you have finished your task.

#### Tip #10: Know when to change jobs

As a self-taught developer, you probably have a sense of loyalty and gratitude to the first company that gave you a chance. However, as a junior developer, you also need to code and practise programming concepts every day. Admittedly, sometimes it's challenging for a team lead to find tasks suitable for a junior developer.

If your company doesn't seem to actually have work for you, if you don't have a mentor, if your manager seems clueless how to grow you into a better developer, if you are given to do mundane tasks over a long period of time (weeks and months), don't be afraid to look for new jobs. Not every dev job is worth staying at.
> ***It isn't just about having the 'software engineer' title on your LinkedIn, it's about progressing and learning every day, or at least most days.***
