In this article, I'm going to discuss exactly what makes a great developer website, GitHub portfolio, and LinkedIn profile. I'll share what to avoid — things that are a waste of time, red flags for employers, or will raise the wrong kinds of questions about your professional experience.

If you're starting from scratch — with just your resume and maybe a few portfolio projects for GitHub — it will take about a month to launch all 3 of these projects. Your website will take the most time. (If you're not a frontend engineer — you might be able to skip a personal website entirely in favor of another portfolio project of your choice.) Your GitHub portfolio is going to be surprisingly easy, since there isn't much you can add to your GitHub profile, and your LinkedIn profile will be almost exactly the same as your resume, except for a few tips that will help you stand out there.

### Why a Website Makes You a Memorable Candidate

Of course, if you're a currently-employed developer with years of experience with a specialist tech stack — then a personal website is definitely not necessary, not even for very senior engineering positions!

So if a personal website is totally unnecessary, why do I strongly recommend you put one together, and now? The reason is because a great personal website can help you catch the eye of hiring managers and be more memorable. Humans are inherently gossipy (as my wife puts it), and who wouldn't want to procrastinate for a few minutes from work by checking out the "personal" website of a developer candidate?

Compared to staring at resume PDFs and GitHub portfolios, looking at someone's actual website is thrilling, and it's a project you have total control over. Additionally, a personal website can be a superb inclusion as part of your career programming portfolio, at least when it's well executed. (If your design skills are especially bad, you may want to pay for a designer or buy something like [the Spotlight template from Tailwind](https://tailwindui.com/templates/spotlight).)

I love helping mentor junior engineers with their resumes, portfolios, personal websites, and job hunt. So you should trust me when I say that there are a lot of terrible resumes, personal websites, and programming portfolios out there, and that the average one probably does more harm than good for the job candidate. But don't worry — I'm here to help you!

One of the most common mistakes in the personal website is thinking that anyone actually cares about *you the person* instead of *you the job candidate*. Including more than 1 paragraph of biography or personal blog entries (with terrible titles like "Sunday Breakfast…") are going to be a big mistake.

I wouldn't even include a blog at all unless you're planning to keep it up to date — I've published 300 Medium articles in 5years and *still* struggle with consistency — because an abandoned blog (or podcast, or Twitch live stream, or YouTube channel, etc.) looks *worse* than not having one at all.

But a well-executed developer website supplemented by a professional LinkedIn profile — preferably with testimonials about your work in one or both places — will make you much more memorable than a LinkedIn alone. And I've seen candidates with great websites withdraw their applications to positions I've been hiring for because they got hired that quickly based on the strength of their developer websites and GitHub portfolio projects!

### What Makes a Great Developer Website Stand Out

The ideal developer website is clean, with great design including a nice custom logo if that's your thing. It may include a dark mode toggle, a fantastic "highlight on hover" link effect, a link to a PDF resume, and tons of real testimonials from colleagues. Many developers choose to highlight portfolio projects right on the page, with a single-page "scrolling" design being common but not necessarily required — though a headshot usually is. You might post your clean, well-organized code on your GitHub, with CI/CD build status and 100% test coverage displayed proudly in badges.

One way to stand out is to give context for your portfolio projects, either on your website or in your projects' `README.md` files. Especially if you don't currently have the right job title due to career change or unemployment, providing this context to your portfolio projects shows technical understanding. Ideally, all of your projects would use a single, consistent tech stack that matches the tech stack for jobs you're applying to, as well.

The best developer websites I've seen have given no indication that they're "out of date" — while sites that include a blog, podcast, YouTube channel, or other media give me a different impression. So you can actually make yourself seem like a stronger candidate (one who has an "updated" website) simply by skipping on publishing blogs or other timestamped media.

Of course, testimonials are the best thing for your website, hands down. It also makes sense to include links to your resume in PDF format, your LinkedIn profile, and/or your GitHub profile. That's why I recommend always updating your website, LinkedIn, and GitHub at the same time.

### 5 Do's, 5 Don'ts, and 5 "Nice To Haves" For Your Site

You'll be in fantastic shape if your developer website fits some or all of the following 5 criteria:

1. A clean, responsive design, preferably one built "mobile first."- A professional headshot, your headline, and a (very) short biography.- Context for your portfolio projects, either on the website or on GitHub.- Your resume and some testimonials, even if you just link to LinkedIn.- Your contact information (and bonus points for a scheduling link).

Even if you don't have the best design, headshot, or writing skills, your website will be much more impressive if you include 3–5 of the above. Something like a Calendly link may not get much use, but it also demonstrates professionalism and time management — you can always send it to tech recruiters who reach out to you via LinkedIn.

Here are 5 things that I recommend you avoid when you build your developer website:

1. Side projects that require ongoing maintenance such as blogs or podcasts.- Anything that's out of date, such as projects using Angular when you now use React.- Too much background about "you the person" instead of "you the job candidate."- Wasting too much time on your personal website, since it doesn't matter *that* much.- Bad design, whether that's an autoplay video\* or just a jarring color scheme.

*\*I think an autoplay background video can work well in certain circumstances, like if you use* *[Tailwind filters](https://v2.tailwindcss.com/docs/filter)* *to reduce its opacity,* *[Typewriter.js](https://safi.me.uk/typewriterjs/)* *for a typing effect, and/or an effect like* *[FullPage.js](https://alvarotrigo.com/fullPage/)**. Otherwise, it's too distracting.*

The idea is that you want your website to be 100% consistent with your resume, which is 100% consistent with your LinkedIn profile. You also want it to look "current" (not "outdated") and to have a modern design. GitHub projects should also be up to date and consistent with your site.

Finally, here are 5 things that are "nice to haves" but that I consider optional for your site:

1. A high-performance website (with the Google Lighthouse scores to prove it).- A website optimized for SEO via the <title>, heading tags, and/or domain name.- A dark mode / light mode toggle, which is always a nice perk but not a necessity.- An accessible website that works great with keyboard (and passes Lighthouse's tests).- Fun animations that add small flourishes to the website but aren't too distracting.

These are things that put developer websites over the top in terms of the top 1–10% of all developer sites. But hiring mangers are unlikely to examine your website's performance, its technical SEO, its keyboard accessibility. So the most impactful of these 5 features would be the dark mode / light mode toggle and any types of animation / motion design.

### Context in Your Portfolio Projects Is Important

Let's talk about providing context for your portfolio projects, because it's a natural transition point between your personal website and your GitHub portfolio. To understand what I mean, you want to put yourself in the role of the job interview, and answer the hiring manager's questions:

* Why did you choose the technologies that you used for this project?* What was the most difficult technical challenge that you had to solve?* Did you experience "feature creep"? If you did, how did you handle it?* What is one thing that you did wrong on this project, and why did you?* What did you learn from this project that will help you succeed in this job?

That's what I mean by providing context to your portfolio project. Assume that the hiring manager is actually going to read what you write (remembering that they're not going to read too much, maybe 1 minute or 200 words maximum). Give him or her a glance inside your head, helping them relate to your thought processes and how you work.

Let them have the opportunity to imagine you succeeding in the role they hire you for, which will help them look good at work as well. Later in the book, we'll discuss the interview process in detail, but you want to be able to preemptively steer the discussion towards the things that your portfolio can't answer on its own, like how you collaborate with others. (Are you a team player? How do you handle conflict?)

Your portfolio should already have provided them with enough depth (context) to understand that you are a great programmer and that you're able to solve complex engineering problems. But, if they *don't* read your portfolio, you still want to have spent some time thinking about context and writing up detailed notes for each of your portfolio projects, either on your website or in your GitHub portfolio's README files. Why? Because you'll be super well-prepared for your technical interviews!

Of course, the best time to write about these types of things is while you're working on your projects. One idea would be to write an abridged summary of the project to use as part of a description of the project on your personal website. So, while you work on your personal website, take the opportunity to keep a technical journal, write up a discussion section, and think about "the optics": how your project is going to look during your job hunt and the application process. Does it convey your expertise?

Personally, I'm a little disappointed any time that I review someone's portfolio and find it lacking in code comments, technical discussion, or best practices. But the good news is that you can make a distinctive impression by including these things throughout your portfolio.

In fact, just putting in an extra 10% effort will put you ahead of most other applicants, which I learned while I was an Airbnb SuperHost.

### This Weird Thing I Learned as an Airbnb SuperHost

When I was an Airbnb SuperHost, I was always amazed at the number of bad listings in my area. Of course, there are listings where someone posted it one time, forgot about it, and it's not an active rental, but there are rentals that look "pretty good" but make every mistake in the book.

The majority of Airbnb hosts don't bother for SuperHost status, use throwaway cell phone pictures that are crooked and dark, and seem to have trouble making their guests feel welcome.

When we saw that, my business partner and I decided to put in an extra 10% effort to every guest's stay. First, we paid a professional photographer to take the photos for the listing. Then, for each guest of legal drinking age, we would leave a handwritten note along with a $5 bottle of wine.

That **10% effort** paid off with **100% higher rental income**, along with numerous 5-star reviews, because "the little things matter," *a lot*.

And you can do the exact same thing as a professional programmer!!!

Put in 10% more effort on your portfolio now, and you'll be the one reviewing 6-figure job offers, while others will still be hunting for a job.

And that takes us to your GitHub profile, where I recommend you pin 3–6 portfolio projects all using the same tech stack to convey your expertise.

### 5 Dos For Your GitHub Developer Profile / Portfolio

Your GitHub profile — which I interchangeably call your GitHub portfolio — is surprisingly simple, since it consists of a headshot, a short headline, a small amount of contact information, and up to 6 "pinned" projects.

(I've found that no one cares about your Twitter or other social media that isn't LinkedIn when hiring you. I wouldn't bother linking it anywhere, unless you have a noteworthy amount of followers, like 10K+.)

Your GitHub profile's pinned projects are your programming portfolio, and you want to keep 5 essential goals in mind when working on yours:

1. Keep your GitHub portfolio up to date by removing old projects when you stop maintaining them or when you change tech stacks.- Don't include your open-source side projects that need ongoing maintenance, because outdated projects make you seem stale.- Use all 6 pins to feature your projects if you can. It's more important to have 3–6 projects than to create a fancy GitHub `README.md` profile file.- Fill out the "description" section and `README.md` file for each of your pinned GitHub portfolio projects. The more context, the better.- Stay professional, including in your username, headshot, and project descriptions. This is your professional brand as an expert developer.

As I mention in #3 above, it's possible to use something called a GitHub [portfolio README](https://docs.github.com/en/account-and-profile/setting-up-and-managing-your-github-profile/customizing-your-profile/managing-your-profile-readme). It's entirely optional to include a portfolio README.

Sometimes, it can make you look generic and take the focus away from your projects. A good portfolio README will be direct and to the point about *why* you're "so good they can't ignore you" with your specialist tech stack. A bad portfolio README will list 20 technologies that you've only ever used for 5 minutes "because the icons look cool."

If you want to include a portfolio README, then keep it professional and short; you can always use your website to "show off."

Other than the "best practices" like organizing your code that you should already be doing, that's really about it for your GitHub portfolio.

### Why I Recommend You Make a Site Instead of Blog

I'll take a short second to do a public service announcement and discourage you from blogging, which may sound funny because I'm "a famous blogger" — or as famous as my follower count makes me, at least.

But there are tons of issues with blogging as a programmer:

* Chiefly that no one actually has the time to blog, work, keep up-to-date on skills, and have a family life. It's inevitable that *any* project like a blog, podcast, or social media presence is going to have periods where you just can't keep it up-to-date, and then it's a liability.* Furthermore, no one who has 10 minutes to screen you as a job candidate has time to actually read your blog.* Finally, hiring managers want specialist software engineers — not generalists who "are technical writers." They want hard skills, full stop.

Unless you're pursuing blogging as a full-time profession, which will be awful for your software engineering career because you will no longer be employed with the "right job title" as a software engineer, avoid blogging.

Even if you're incredibly consistent about publishing at least 1x article a month, without ever stopping — 90% of the time it becomes a burden.

Theoretically, you could invest 1–2 months and schedule out 24 articles to publish once a month for the next 2 years. That would give you an active blog, but there just really isn't any point. It's too much to keep up with.

As soon as you stop publishing for 3 months, or 6 months, or 12 months, or a few years — your blog looks like curdled milk on your developer website. Hiring managers are not the least bit interested, and you could have invested that time in improving your website or portfolio projectsm more.

Let's wrap up with your LinkedIn profile.

### What Makes an Amazing LinkedIn Developer Profile

Last but not least, we have your LinkedIn profile. Your personal website is less important than your LinkedIn profile, and the portfolio is somewhere in between the other two. In other words, your LinkedIn is your #1 most important piece of web presence; your portfolio is #2; and your personal site is #3. The good news about your LinkedIn is that it's almost exactly the same as your resume, but the bad news is that you have to meticulously copy-paste your entire resume over to LinkedIn by hand. And you have to update it every time you update [your 6-figure resume](https://medium.com/p/c4f556538e66).

But the reason LinkedIn is so important is that your best chance of getting a 6-figure programming job is through a technical recruiter who reaches out to you first. The "technical" in technical recruiter doesn't mean much, just that they recruit for "technical" jobs (software engineering jobs), so don't expect them to understand programming technologies in any depth, like why I specialize in Tailwind CSS. In fact, they usually hardly know anything at all about programming, which is not great. But recruiters are paid when they get *you* a job, and I've successfully used them many times — so the best approach is to treat them with patience, kindness, and grace.

The idea with having a complete LinkedIn profile is that you want to optimize for being found in searches — so you want to "go in" on things like the job title you want and what skills you specialize in as part of your headline in your profile. There's a headshot, a headline, and some meaningless sections ("Featured" posts and your "Activity" on LinkedIn), followed by an editable "About" section where I recommend you list your website and portfolio URLs along with your short description, Areas of Expertise, Key Skills, and Technical Skills from your updated resume.

The next section is your work "Experience," which is basically the exact same as your resume. Your current or most recent jobs go at the top, and then the others are listed in reverse chronological order. I use the exact same text as my resume, replacing the bullets from my Google Doc with copy-pasted bullet characters (• — just google "bullet point"). Only your 5 most recent jobs will make it onto the list without requiring another click, and that's probably a good rule in general for your resume; your experience more than 5 jobs ago may not be super useful.

Then, we have "Education," which is self-explanatory, and a "Licenses & certifications" section that you'll probably leave blank unless you're some type of Certified Cloud Developer™️. The next section, "Volunteering," will probably also be blank, though of course it's fine if you have something to include there. After that, there are two sections that are unique to LinkedIn and probably worth your time to fill out: "Skills" and "Recommendations." You can attach "Skills" to each of your jobs, and you can also get "endorsements" from other LinkedIn users, but neither of those is especially useful for helping you get a job. If you want to do something extra, I recommend you spend a few hours taking up to 3 "LinkedIn Skill Assessments," which are self-paced LinkedIn quizzes. You'll get a little icon for these, and they can help a bit in proving your technical skills. (Unfortunately, companies will often still make you take "their" assessment.)

The "Recommendations" section is just a fancy name for testimonials that you can solicit from other LinkedIn users. You will have to solicit them, since almost no one will bother to write you a testimonial (for anything in your life) unless you ask for it directly, and maybe bribe them with cookies. You can only show the 3 most recent recommendations, though like testimonials it never hurts to have more; [career coach Mark Langford has 82 recommendations on his profile](https://www.linkedin.com/in/marklangford/details/recommendations/?detailScreenTabIndex=0). Just like customer reviews, recommendations really help you stand out and prove your worth, whether they're from colleagues, clients, friends, or all of the above. Take the time to ask for some, and you'll be well rewarded for the effort. (This is a great reason to work with clients!)

The "Recommendations" section is really the only part of your LinkedIn profile that's any different from your resume, which is why I think all the rest of the sections are pretty much a waste of time. For completeness, I'll mention that there are several additional sections: "Publications," "Honors & awards," "Test scores," "Languages," and "Interests" (companies you like on LinkedIn). As you can guess, only the "Languages" section might be worth filling out, and even then it should better say at least "Professional working proficiency" (or preferably "Native or bilingual proficiency") in English (or whichever language you plan to use all day at work). [Bet](https://www.urbandictionary.com/define.php?term=Bet).

### Wrapping Up: Developer Site, GitHub, and LinkedIn

To create an impactful online presence as a developer, blogging isn't a good investment of your time. You should focus on making a professional website, a world-class GitHub portfolio, and a searchable LinkedIn profile.

Avoid outdated projects and unnecessary personal or biographical details, as these mistakes will make you seem unprofessional or "too junior."

Do include context in your portfolio projects, present them professionally, and make sure they use technologies related to ones you use at work.

By investing 10% extra effort into your professional brand as an expert software engineer, you will boost your appeal to hiring managers and stand out in this competitive job market. **Happy coding!** 😁
