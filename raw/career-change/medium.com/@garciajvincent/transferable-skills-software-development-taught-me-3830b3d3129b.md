Before personally diving into Software Development, I often thought of a programmer as a Tech-Savy genius with narrow interests. In other words, a genius savant who's defined solely on their technical expertise. This assumption was debunked when I became a professional Software Developer and discovered diversity in the tech industry. I found more programmers who had hobbies outside of programming including cooking, photography, blogging, and playing a musical instrument. These programmers credit Software Development for providing them the ability to enjoy their hobbies. This had me thinking, "***What skills outside of coding have Software Development helped me developed?****".* This was not a simple question for me to answer at first since I spend the majority of my time practicing Software Development. After a meditation session, it began to click how many traits from Software Development I apply in my everyday life. **In this article, I provide 5 transferable skills that Software Development taught me because of 1.) It sounds more official and 2.) I intend to make a Part 2 as I experience more of what life has to offer.**

### **1. Breaking Problems to Its Smallest Components:**

Problem-solving is a critical skill in Software Development. In my opinion, 90% of development is solving problems and 10% actually writing the code. I want to take this skill a step-further by mentioning that problem-solving is achieved by breaking down problems to the smallest component. When taking on a new project, the long list of tasks to perform can be overwhelming. On top of the long list of tasks, you will often encounter bugs in the process. There are plenty of moments where I would spend hours to work around one bug. It was always a rewarding experience when I am able to successfully Google my solution or explore the Docs.

Elementary

***How is this skill transferable?***

I apply my ability to break down problems to the smallest components during a confrontation. Rather than get caught into a shouting match, I ask open-ended to move the conversation forward. My favorite quote to summarize how I approach confrontation is: "*You want to be right, or do it right?".* In other words, I do my best to keep an open mind even if I don't agree with what the other person is saying. I understand that everyone's belief system is based on the content they consume, which is a topic I will explore later in this article. If I want to progress the conversation and reach a consensus with the person I am debating with, I will have to find the root cause of their intentions and belief system.

### **2. Emotional Control:**

I often hear that Programming is an activity that centers around logic. Based on my personal experience, this claim is true if I want to be productive. When debugging it is easy to get overwhelmed with frustration that your code is not working as intended. Frustration will lead to doubt of one's own ability to be a developer and, if not checked, can lead to quitting software development as a career! To anyone eager to start a career in software development, I do not want that outcome for anyone who is willing to put in the work! Managing one's emotions is a critical skill to increase productivity and shut-off doubt. I picture this skill as going Ultra Instinct or as most people may call, *[Flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_(psychology))*.

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*P50WfYvhiRpUr2weoTYBuA.gif)

Mastered Ultra Instinct

***How is this skill transferable?***

This could be because of my personality type, but I notice my best decisions were made through controlling my own emotions and finding opportunity in every situation. There is a contradicting claim that some of the best decisions are made through emotion because logic can support fear, however, overcoming fear is a part of controlling emotions. If I had made decisions based off of the fear of missing out, I would not make the time to focus on being a better developer on weekends in favor of binge-watching the latest series on Netflix or going out to the latest venue. If I had made decisions based on the fear of failure, I would not have the opportunity to experience NeighborNosh Inc grow as a company and gain professional experience. If I had made decisions out of fear for what others thought of me, I would be a miserable Biochemistry major pursuing a career I knew would not provide me satisfaction and limit opportunities for growth. Managing my own emotions provide me the ability to apply the Stoic Mindset and seek opportunities for growth no matter my past decisions.

### **3. Perseverance/Grit:**

Expanding off the skill of controlling emotions, perseverance is essential for overcoming blockers in projects. Again, the major source of this stress comes from spending hours or days on a handful of bugs. I often read/hear stories from Reddit and in-person that too many junior developers quit programming because they find the task "too hard" or not how they imagine. Based on my short life, most junior developers will have to grind for months studying and receiving rejections before earning the first gig. ***Often the first year as a developer will not involve a six-figure salary, beer at the office, an abundance of food in stock with Kombucha, and sitting at a hipster style office with A/C.***Most developers will have to endure the long-haul of understanding their chosen stack and 100+ rejection emails to reap the rewards of being a professional Software Developer. There is a reason why Software Development is a high demand field and future developers should prepare to **[Git Gud](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/git-gud)**!

Dark Souls is a game infamous for its difficulty and the Git Gud meme.

***How is this skill transferable?***

I first experience perseverance hands-on as a developer when I began my journey with both Thinkful and NeighborNosh Inc. I was not making much money at NeighborNosh Inc in the beginning due to it being an early-stage startup with not much funding, so I had to work odd jobs to increase my savings. In addition to working a part-time odd job and NeighborNosh Inc, I attended Thinkful part-time so I can build the foundation for me to learn the React library to apply at NeighborNosh Inc. I often encountered blockers and bugs left-and-right when it came to making UI changes on a stack I was not familiar with or integrating a new Backend application to render an API.

Outside of programming, perseverance is a valuable skill for discovering one's higher purpose and not allow life to pass by every day. ***It is usually the hard things that are the most beneficial and the easy things that are the most harmful.*** For example, eating a whole pizza from Dominos with a liter of Coke tastes better than having to eat a plain salad with a cup of water, but the former will most likely result in obesity if consumed often. It is strange how life works like this.

### **4. Autonomy/Working without Motivation:**

No, I do not mean making a username irrelevant to one's actual name with an anime or a dead meme as the profile picture. I mean the ability to work and achieve goals through one's own will alone. I agree with the idea that seeking help when needed and mentorship will quickly improve one's ability to be a professional developer and achieve goals at life. I, however, often see job postings where a desirable skill is to work autonomously with little to no supervision. There will be instances when working on a project where the programmer working on the project knows the error better than someone looking into the bug without context. This is where working to solve the problem without outside help will come in handy. One will improve their Google skills and ability to connect previous experiences to work around a problem. Again, ***I find the importance of seeking help when needed, but there are greater rewards when developers are able to solve problems on their own without having a coach constantly cheer them***.

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*p6opyUH723XudKwSdm16Sw.gif)

Eye of the Tiger

***How is this skill transferable?***

***Working without motivation or a coach always on the stand-lines is an incredible skill to achieve not just in Software Development but in life.*** I often had the nagging voice in my head that said "*React is too hard, can't we stick with jQuery"* or "*I don't understand Unit Testing, can't I just ship my code and hope for the best!"*. **This was the lazy version of me that wanted to take shortcuts and focused too much on the results rather than the process**. If I had listened to the lazy version of me, I would have made the code on my projects ugly. If I had listened to the lazy version of me, I would not be able to finish my first Node and React projects. If I had listened to the lazy version of me, I would not have the right to call myself a Full-Stack Engineer. ***To reach my highest potential, I had to manifest the lazy version of me and learn to shut him off when things get tough.*** I had to expect that no white-knight will come and save me. Adopting the ability to work without motivation changed my outlook on obstacles! ***There is no such thing as failure, there is only growth! I will either win or grow stronger through experience! No motivation is required!***

### **5. Filtering:**

There is an abundance of information given social media and the internet. Other developers and I struggled with the issue on which programming language to learn given the abundance of tools to use. I found another tough decision for most programmers is to decide on one language to study before jumping to other languages. Most programming languages such as Javascript and Python are built in the same fundamentals. I understand that new developers are eager to learn as many languages as possible because a lot of job postings state 10+ languages/tools. ***The reality is that no one fits the role/job description 100%***. Unless the job description states "Candidate must know how to print 'Hello World' in 10 scripting languages" there is no point in taking the shotgun approach to software development. That is why it is vital for developers to pick their niche on the first day of development and find what technical stack is most relevant for their niche.

![None](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:700/1*SOJwNnj_oMNa8BsXKE6uyg.gif)

I was guilty of this back in college.

***How is this skill transferable?***

A great video I watched from the [TechLead](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PzNMOgvZxF4) convinced me to stop watching/reading mainstream news, politics, and social justice. I do not picture myself being a world leader, so I saw little value in keeping up with news related to politics or the latest hashtag trending on Twitter. I picture myself being a Chief Technology Officer so I focus on strictly technology and business. I agree with staying aware of politics through the economic lens, but I find it more valuable to filter information that is relevant to one's needs. ***I know my circumstances and goals more than anybody!***

This is how I encourage someone reading this article to think about this skill. Who earns a higher income, a family doctor or a neurosurgeon? The answer, in most cases, a neurosurgeon because they specialize in a specific field that creates a high value. Rather than being a generalist in medicine, the neurosurgeon is able to charge a higher price for being a specialist. There is some value in being a generalist if one's goal is to be in a multi-disciplinary business or achieve a Renaissance status. Before seeking out different disciplines, I find it more valuable to focus on one subject for a few months before being able to move on to other disciplines.

Those were the 5 skills I found transferable that I learned from Software Development. I hope readers found this article useful and are more encouraged to pursue software development.
