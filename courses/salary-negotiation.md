# Course: Salary Negotiation for Tech Roles

**Audience:** developers and engineers fielding offers (first job, lateral, or senior)
**Format:** 5 modules, ~3 lessons each, micro-course style
**Through-line:** *Negotiation starts before the first call and ends in writing. Most candidates lose money in three places: pre-screen anchor, the counter call, and the equity/benefits line items they didn't know they could ask about.*

## Module 1 — Before the Offer: Research and Anchoring
**Objective:** Do the homework that gives you a defensible number before anyone asks.

- Three data sources to triangulate: levels.fyi for total comp at named companies, Glassdoor / Blind for ranges, recent posts from people in your exact role on LinkedIn.
- Total compensation is not just base salary: base + bonus + equity (RSUs or options) + sign-on + benefits. A $150k base with $50k equity is materially different from $180k base with no equity.
- Build a "walk-away number" — the minimum you'd accept — and a "target number" — what you'd say yes to without further negotiation. Know both before any conversation.
- Geographic bands matter for remote roles. SF/NYC bands can be 30–50% higher than secondary metros for the same title.

**Action:** Open a doc and fill in three rows for your target role: levels.fyi median, Glassdoor median, walk-away number. Save it.

## Module 2 — The Recruiter Phone Screen: Handling Salary Expectations
**Objective:** Survive the first salary question without anchoring yourself below market.

- The recruiter will ask about expectations — sometimes in the screen, sometimes later. The wrong answer here can cap your final offer.
- Default move: deflect to "I'd like to learn more about the role before sharing a number" — buys you time, doesn't anchor low.
- If pressed, give a range based on your research, with the low end above your walk-away number. Never give a single number.
- Ask their range first if you can: "What's the budgeted range for this role?" Many recruiters will share it.
- "What are you making now?" is increasingly illegal to ask in many jurisdictions — and irrelevant. Redirect to expectations.

**Action:** Write your verbatim script for the salary expectations question. Practice saying it out loud three times. It should fit in under 20 seconds.

## Module 3 — Receiving the Offer: What to Do in the First 24 Hours
**Objective:** Buy time, get the full picture, never accept on the call.

- Never accept on the call. The right response: "Thank you so much — I'm excited. Can I take 24–48 hours to review the full details?"
- Get everything in writing before negotiating: base, bonus structure, equity (with vesting schedule and strike price for options), sign-on, benefits, start date, working arrangement.
- Calculate total comp year 1 vs year 4. Equity that cliffs at year 1 reads very differently from equity that's front-loaded.
- Identify what's negotiable: base (always), sign-on (usually), equity (often), start date (almost always), title (sometimes), remote arrangement (depends).

**Action:** Build a one-page offer-comparison template with rows for base, year-1 bonus, equity (year 1, year 4 total), sign-on, benefits. Use it next time you have an offer.

## Module 4 — The Counter: Scripts and What to Ask For
**Objective:** Negotiate the gap between their offer and your target without burning the relationship.

- The counter conversation script: appreciate the offer, name your target number, anchor with one specific reason (competing offer, market data, scope of role).
- Negotiate base first (multiplier effect on every future raise), then equity, then sign-on. Sign-on is one-time; base compounds.
- Ranges to ask for: 10–20% above the initial offer for base, 30–50% more equity, 1.5–2x the offered sign-on if any.
- Things beyond comp: title (affects future market value), start date, remote arrangement, additional PTO, professional development budget, equipment.
- Silence is a tactic. After you state your counter, stop talking. Let them respond.

**Action:** Draft your counter-offer script for a hypothetical 10% gap between offer and target. Include one specific anchor reason and the exact words you'll use to ask.

## Module 5 — Closing: Multi-Offer Leverage and Walking Away
**Objective:** Get to a signed offer letter that reflects what was verbally agreed.

- Multi-offer leverage doubles your bargaining power. If you have a real competing offer, name the company and number — don't bluff with a fake one. If you don't, focus on market data and role fit.
- The "exploding offer" tactic (deadline within 48 hours) is mostly a pressure move. Push back politely: "I want to give this the consideration it deserves. Can we extend to end of next week?"
- Walking away is real leverage. If the final offer doesn't meet your walk-away number after a good-faith counter, decline cleanly. Don't bluff if you wouldn't actually walk.
- Get the final terms in writing in the offer letter — not just email. Read every line before signing. Verbal promises about future raises or remote arrangements rarely survive a manager change.

**Action:** Write a one-paragraph "walk-away" script you could read aloud politely if a final offer fell below your minimum. Save it.
