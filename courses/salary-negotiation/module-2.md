# Module 2 — The Recruiter Phone Screen: Handling Salary Expectations

**Objective:** Survive the first salary question without anchoring below market.

---

## Lesson 1: Why the First Number You Say Can Cap Your Entire Offer

The recruiter phone screen feels like a casual conversation, but the moment a salary question enters it, the dynamic shifts. That one question — "What are your salary expectations?" — is not small talk. It is, as one negotiation resource puts it bluntly, "a tactical maneuver. It is a trap designed to anchor you to the lowest possible number."[^1]

Anchoring is a cognitive bias: the first number stated in a negotiation exerts disproportionate gravity over every number that follows. "If you state your expectation is $80,000, you will never get an offer for $100,000, even if that was their budgeted amount."[^1]

This happens earlier than most candidates expect. "Your salary negotiation could literally begin with your very first conversation with someone at the company. That first call is often called the 'pre-screen' or 'pre-interview,' and you'll usually talk with an internal recruiter at the company."[^2] By the time you receive a formal offer, the number you mentioned on that fifteen-minute call has already shaped the room.

The stakes compound when your current pay enters the picture. "Your previous salary anchors downward. Many employers will offer a percentage increase (like 10–15%) over your current salary, locking you into lower compensation."[^3] One number said once, weeks before an offer is made, can define the ceiling of your compensation for years.

The good news: you are not obligated to answer. Your best move at this stage is to say as little as possible while keeping the conversation warm and professional.

**Action:** Before your next phone screen, write down your walk-away number — the minimum you would accept — and commit to never saying any figure below it. Then practice saying nothing at all.

---

## Lesson 2: The Default Deflect — and What to Do When They Push

The most reliable way to protect your leverage on a phone screen is to redirect the salary question entirely. Here is the core deflect script to internalize:

> "I'd like to learn more about the role before sharing a number. I'm confident that if this is the right fit, we'll be able to agree on something competitive and fair."

This phrasing works because it is honest — early in the process, you genuinely do not have enough information to name a fair number. "The role might have responsibilities that weren't in the job posting. The benefits might be worth $10,000 on their own. You need the full picture."[^4]

A longer version that explicitly redirects from current salary: "I'm not comfortable sharing my current salary. I would prefer to focus on the value I can add to this company rather than what I'm paid at my current job. I don't have a specific number in mind for a desired salary, and you know better than I do what value my skillset and experience could bring to your company. I want this move to be a big step forward for me in terms of both responsibility and compensation."[^2]

Recruiters are trained to push. When they do, the instinct is to capitulate — to say anything that ends the awkward silence. Resist it. "Your silence is your greatest asset."[^1]

If the recruiter escalates ("We need a number to proceed"), flip the question back: "I understand you need to ensure I'm within the right bracket, but I'm still learning the full scope of the role. Could you share the budgeted salary range for this position? That would help us confirm we're aligned."[^1] This move is not evasive; it is efficient. You are asking for the information that would actually make the conversation productive.

Three Muse-endorsed strategies, ordered by preference for the phone screen stage: delay answering until you know more about the role, flip the question by asking for their range first, or give a range only if genuinely pressed.[^5]

**Action:** Write out your deflect script in your own voice and say it aloud three times before your next call. Rehearsal removes the hesitation that makes deflection feel rude in the moment.

---

## Lesson 3: If You Must Give a Number — Use a Range, Never a Single Figure

Sometimes deflection is not enough. The recruiter will not advance you without at least a bracket. In that situation, the rule is simple: give a range, never a single number, and make sure the floor of that range is above your true walk-away.

Career coach Jennifer Fink's guidance: "Give a salary range. But keep the bottom of your stated range toward the mid-to-high point of what you're actually hoping for."[^5] This is the key insight. A range feels flexible and reasonable to the recruiter, but if calibrated correctly, its low end still sits above what you would actually accept. The company anchors to your floor; you want that floor to be high.

Word choice inside a range matters too. Notice the difference between "I'd be happy with $95,000–$110,000" versus "I'm targeting $95,000–$110,000." The first signals a ceiling. The second signals a direction.[^4] Use "targeting." Never use "happy with" or "hoping for."

One practical framing for senior roles: "When stating an acceptable salary range be sure the lower number is acceptable to you."[^6] The recruiter will hear the low end as your real number. Design it accordingly.

Finally: do not let your current salary be the starting point. "Even when they ask, never talk about your old salary, except if it was exceptionally above market. Your old salary can only act as an anchor point, and then it's very difficult to get more than 10% above your old salary. If you don't mention your old salary, everything is possible."[^7]

In many places, they are not permitted to ask. "In fact, in many U.S. states, it's illegal to ask about current or past salary. It's okay to say you prefer not to share."[^8] Asking about salary history is now prohibited for some or all employers in jurisdictions including New York City, California, Massachusetts, and others.[^5] Your past pay is irrelevant to your market value in a new role. Treat the question as such, redirect to the role, and move forward.

**Action:** Using market data from Glassdoor, Payscale, or LinkedIn Salaries, determine a range for your target role where the low end sits at or above your walk-away. Write it down. Practice delivering it with the word "targeting" — not "hoping for."

---

## Module Summary

The recruiter phone screen is the earliest and most underestimated moment in a salary negotiation. The first number spoken anchors every dollar that follows. Your default is to deflect: ask to learn more before discussing compensation, and when pushed, flip the question by asking for the company's budgeted range. If a number becomes unavoidable, give a researched range whose floor exceeds your walk-away — and never let your current pay enter the conversation. Protecting your leverage at this stage costs nothing and is worth thousands.
