# Module 3 — Receiving the Offer: What to Do in the First 24 Hours

The offer call is the most emotionally charged moment in the hiring process. Every instinct says to accept immediately and lock it in. That instinct will cost you money. This module covers exactly what to do in the window between hearing the number and committing to anything.

---

## Lesson 3.1 — Never Accept on the Call

The moment a recruiter says "we'd like to offer you the position," the negotiation clock starts. Most candidates lose ground in the next thirty seconds by either accepting outright or blurting out a counter-number before they have any information to negotiate from.

The rule is simple: do not accept, reject, or counter on the spot. Express genuine enthusiasm and buy time. "When you get that call, assuming the offer is within your salary expectations, be mentally prepared to give a non-answer and get off the phone so that you can think properly."[^1]

The phrase that does the work: "Thank you so much — I'm really excited about this opportunity and the team. I'd love to take a couple of days to review the full package. Can you send over the details in writing?"[^2] "You expressed enthusiasm without accepting. You bought time to prepare your counter. You signaled sophistication without being difficult."[^3]

Two to three business days is standard and fully acceptable. Companies will push back as if there is urgency — there almost never is. "Companies will push you to accept an offer but in reality they can wait longer because interviewing another candidate is costlier than waiting a few days for you to accept their offer."[^4]

If the offer comes in lower than expected, signal that briefly — express the number is lower than anticipated, that you are still excited, and that you would like to review the full package before continuing. You have put the company on notice without closing anything.

**Action:** Write down your time-buying phrase before the offer call and rehearse it out loud. Your goal on the call is to end it warmly and with nothing accepted.

---

## Lesson 3.2 — Get Everything in Writing Before You Evaluate Anything

The verbal offer summary you hear on the phone is not the offer. It is a highlight reel. Companies often lead with base salary and leave bonuses, equity details, start date, and benefits for the written document — or skip them entirely on the call. "Sometimes, when they tell you all the numbers on the call, they won't tell you all the details right off the bat."[^1]

Before forming any opinion, get the full written package: base salary, bonus structure and targets, equity details (shares, vesting schedule, cliff, strike price for options, most recent 409A), sign-on, benefits, PTO, start date, and working arrangement. "You want to make sure to see the terms and conditions of the job and know what the compensation looks like before accepting. You can say yes on the phone and in an offer letter see something you had no idea would be in there."[^5] "Write it all down. Doesn't matter if they're going to send you a written version later, write everything down."[^6]

Once you have the written offer, build a complete total compensation picture before reacting to any single line item. Calculate year-one total comp (base + expected bonus + sign-on if any) and year-four total comp with equity fully vested, to understand what staying actually pays. "We recommend plugging all your inputs into a total compensation calculator to determine your package's actual value across all the components. It also accounts for taxes, which can be important because bonuses are taxed higher than the base salary."[^1]

Pay particular attention to how equity vests. The most common schedule is 25 percent per year over four years with a one-year cliff.[^7] Front-loaded vesting is worth more than back-loaded vesting; a $100,000 grant that gives 40 percent in year one reads very differently from a standard cliff. Also confirm the vesting commencement date: "make sure it will reflect the true start date of when you joined the company, not the time at which the stock option is granted."[^8] For options, the strike price matters as much as share count.

**Action:** Build a four-column spreadsheet (years one through four) with base, bonus, vesting equity, and sign-on. The column totals are your actual comp per year. Do not negotiate until this exists.

---

## Lesson 3.3 — Know What Is Negotiable Before You Negotiate

Most candidates treat salary as the only lever. It is actually one of several, and not always the easiest to move. Before you negotiate, map the full landscape of what companies can and will adjust.

Base salary is always on the table. "You shouldn't accept the first salary offer, as it typically prevents you from negotiating for a better offer down the road."[^9] Sign-on is usually easier to move than base — it is a one-time cost that does not affect salary budgets. Equity is negotiable at many companies, especially in tech. Start date is almost always flexible. Title is sometimes negotiable and matters for future market positioning. Remote or hybrid arrangement is increasingly open and can have real dollar value.

"If you only negotiate on salary, you'll be leaving a large amount of your negotiating power off the table. It's crucial that you not only look at every component of a compensation package or offer, but also negotiate on as many points as possible if you want to maximize your opportunity."[^10] Different components have different costs to the employer. A company with a hard salary band may have full flexibility on a sign-on bonus. "Rank components by personal value and likelihood of employer flexibility: items with low employer cost but high personal value (remote work, flexible schedule), one-time costs not affecting ongoing budget (signing bonus, relocation assistance), performance-based compensation, then base salary increases."[^11]

You do not need to raise every point simultaneously — but think through each one before the call so that if base is immovable you are not flat-footed. "Once you get an offer in writing, reflect on what else may be negotiable, including the job title, start date, a moving stipend, a sign-on or retention bonus, tuition assistance and more."[^5]

**Action:** Before your negotiation call, write down every component in the offer and mark each one: must improve, nice to improve, or acceptable as-is. Rank the "must improve" items. You will lead with your top one or two and hold the others in reserve as potential trade-offs.

---

## Module Summary

The first 24 hours after receiving an offer are for information gathering, not committing. Three rules govern this window: never accept on the call — buy time with a warm, enthusiastic non-answer; get every component in writing before forming any opinion; and map the full negotiation landscape before you counter. Base salary is not the only lever, and the company's first number in any category is rarely their last. Treat the written offer as the start of a conversation, not the end of one.
