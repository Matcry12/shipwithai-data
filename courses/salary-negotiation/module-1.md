# Module 1 — Before the Offer: Research and Anchoring

**Objective:** Do the homework that gives you a defensible number before anyone asks.

---

## Lesson 1: Triangulate Your Data

Single-source salary research is a liability. Any one database reflects a slice of the market — a particular company tier, a particular survey methodology, a particular year. The goal is to arrive at a number you can defend with multiple independent data points, so that when a recruiter asks "where did you get that figure?" you have three credible answers, not one.

For tech roles, start with levels.fyi. Unlike general-purpose salary sites, it reports total compensation broken down by level and company, which means you can compare like-for-like rather than mixing apples and oranges. "Glassdoor, Salary.com, PayScale, and Levels.fyi (particularly for tech roles) provide salary data filtered by job title, location, company size, and experience level."[^1] Cross-reference Glassdoor for ranges at companies you are actually targeting, and check Blind for candid posts from current employees — the signal-to-noise ratio is higher than general forums because posters are verified employees.

LinkedIn is the underused third leg. "LinkedIn Salary Insights offers compensation data based on member-reported information."[^1] Beyond the formal Salary Insights tool, recent LinkedIn posts from engineers describing their offers or sharing compensation data (these appear regularly in the feed and in communities) give you real-time market color that no database captures immediately.

The synthesis step matters as much as the collection step. "Use at least three" sources, and "aim for the 50th to 75th percentile" — the median is for candidates with no differentiators.[^2] If you have specialized skills, relevant certifications, or a strong portfolio, you belong in the upper half of the range. Write down the numbers from each source, note the date, and identify where they cluster. That cluster is your anchor range.

One structural nuance levels.fyi makes explicit: leveling differs across companies. "There is not a standardized leveling system across tech companies. Meaning a Level 5 Software Engineer at Google does not necessarily have the same level of responsibility and influence as a E5 at Facebook."[^3] Always verify the level mapping before drawing comparisons, or you will walk into a negotiation with a number that reflects a more senior role than the one on offer.

**Action:** Open levels.fyi, Glassdoor, and LinkedIn Salary Insights. For your target role and two companies comparable to the one you are interviewing with, record base, bonus, and equity figures in a spreadsheet. Note the date of each data point. Identify the 50th–75th percentile band. That band is your working research range for this module.

## Lesson 2: Understand Total Compensation Before You Compare Anything

The single most common research mistake is comparing base salaries as if they represent the full picture. They do not. "The 'salary' a company offers is incomplete. It's not lying — just incomplete."[^4]

Total compensation follows the formula: **base + bonus + equity + sign-on + benefits**. Each component behaves differently, vests on different timelines, and carries different risk. "Many engineers only see the base salary of 200,000 and miss the other 300,000."[^4] A real Silicon Valley example from the same source: a $200k base, $20–40k annual bonus, and $80k/year in RSUs produces a total compensation in the $480–500k range — more than double what the base number suggests.

The components break down as follows per levels.fyi's compensation guide:

- **Base salary** is guaranteed cash paid every period. It is the hardest component to negotiate because it affects salary bands permanently.
- **Annual bonus** is paid once per year and tied to performance targets. "Bonus percentages are usually non-negotiable and standard per level."[^3]
- **Equity** (RSUs at public companies, options at startups) grants partial ownership and vests over time, typically four years. Its value depends entirely on company trajectory.
- **Signing bonus** is a one-time payment — "often ranging from $1,000 to $100,000 depending on seniority and role" — and is frequently the most negotiable element of the package.[^3]

Why this matters for research: a $150k base + $50k in annual RSU vesting is not the same as a $180k base + $0 equity, even though the annual cash looks similar. The equity offer has upside that evaporates entirely if you leave before the four-year cliff, or compounds dramatically if the company grows. You cannot evaluate an offer, or compare it to market data, until you have converted everything into an annualized total comp number using the same method for each comparison.

"Account for total compensation, not just base salary — benefits, bonuses, equity, and perks significantly impact your true earnings."[^1] One concrete illustration of how large this difference can be: an $80k base salary with standard benefits, bonus, 401k match, and stock options totals roughly $114,854 in real compensation. "This 43% difference between base salary and total compensation highlights why comprehensive evaluation matters for negotiation strategy."[^5]

**Action:** Take the research range you built in Lesson 1 and rebuild it as total compensation numbers. For each data point, identify what the equity grant was (in annualized dollars), add any reported bonus, and note whether the comparison figure included benefits. If levels.fyi reports a total comp of $X, verify what components are included. Your target range and walk-away number (Lesson 3) must be stated in total comp terms, not base alone.

## Lesson 3: Define Your Two Numbers Before Any Conversation Starts

Most candidates enter salary conversations knowing only a vague sense of what they want. That is a structural disadvantage. You need to arrive with two precise, pre-committed numbers: a walk-away number and a target number.

The walk-away number is the floor — "the minimum you'd accept."[^6] Below this number, the economics do not work: your cost of living, your current total comp, your career trajectory, or your lifestyle requirements make acceptance irrational. You set this number privately and never share it. It exists so that in a live negotiation, under social pressure, you cannot be talked below it without knowing you crossed the line.

The target number is what you would accept without negotiating further — the outcome that makes you genuinely satisfied. This is distinct from your opening ask. "Define your desired salary and your minimum acceptable salary (your walk-away number), but do not disclose this to the company."[^7] The target is what you orient the negotiation toward; the opening ask should be anchored above it.

Anchoring is the mechanism that makes your opening number matter. "Negotiation is in many ways a game, and it takes two to play. If you are offered $100k and ask for $150k, the company is likely to come back with a number that's somewhere in the middle. Avoid that hassle by shooting a little higher than you actually want."[^8] Anchoring higher pulls the midpoint of any counter-offer closer to your true target.

A complete pre-negotiation number set therefore has three entries: walk-away (private floor), target (your real goal), and opening ask (anchored above target). "Calculate your target salary by determining three numbers: your minimum acceptable (walk-away point), your target (what you'd be happy with), and your optimistic ask (ambitious but defensible). When presenting your range, lead with your target-to-optimistic range, allowing room for negotiation while anchoring higher."[^1]

Geographic bands add one more variable for remote roles. Location significantly affects pay: "Higher Cost-of-Living (COL) locations will yield higher compensation packages and vice versa."[^3] For fully remote positions, companies typically set pay to the candidate's metro rather than headquarters. SF and NYC roles routinely run 30–50% above secondary metros for the same title and level. If you are based in a secondary market but being hired by an SF-headquartered team, find out their location-pay policy before you set your numbers — some companies pay SF rates everywhere, others localize aggressively. That policy alone can shift your target range by tens of thousands of dollars.

**Action:** Write down three numbers right now: your walk-away (total comp), your target (total comp), and your opening ask (total comp). For remote roles, look up the company's location-pay policy and adjust all three if necessary. Store these numbers somewhere you can reference before your next conversation with a recruiter. Do not share the walk-away number with anyone at the company.

---

## Module Summary

Negotiation outcomes are determined before the conversation starts, not during it. This module covers the three tasks that give you a defensible position: building a multi-source, triangulated salary range using levels.fyi, Glassdoor, Blind, and LinkedIn; converting every data point into total compensation (base + bonus + equity + sign-on + benefits) so you are comparing like-for-like; and committing to a private walk-away number and a target number before any recruiter asks. Geographic bands for remote roles — typically 30–50% above secondary metros in SF/NYC markets — must be factored into all three numbers. Candidates who complete this research arrive with data, not opinions, and that difference is audible the moment salary comes up.
