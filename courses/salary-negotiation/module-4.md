# Module 4 — The Counter: Scripts and What to Ask For

**Objective:** Negotiate the gap without burning the relationship.

---

## Lesson 1: The Counter Script — Three Sentences That Do All the Work

Most candidates over-complicate the counter. They rehearse elaborate justifications, build multi-slide decks in their heads, and then either blurt out everything at once or say nothing at all. The research is clear: a prepared script, delivered simply, dramatically outperforms improvisation.

The structure is three beats:

1. **Appreciate the offer** — sincerely, not perfunctorily.
2. **Name your target number** — one specific figure, not a hedged range.
3. **Anchor with ONE reason** — your single strongest market or value data point.

Here is what that sounds like in practice:

> *"Thank you so much for the offer — I'm genuinely excited about this role and the team. Based on my research for a [Role Title] with [your key experience] in this market, the competitive range is closer to [$X]. Given my [one specific, quantified achievement], I'm confident I can deliver that value from day one. Would there be flexibility to get to $[target]?"*

[^1]

Notice what is absent: apology, lengthy explanation, or multiple reasons. "Your counteroffer must be presented as a logical business case, not an emotional plea."[^2] One anchor is more persuasive than three because it forces the conversation to engage with a single, strong point rather than letting the other side pick apart a list.

On the specific number: research shows that "$127,500 is more credible than 'around $130K.'"[^3] Odd, precise figures signal you have done real math, not simply rounded up from wishful thinking.

After you deliver the counter, stop talking. Silence is not awkward — it is a tactic. "The pause signals you're not desperate."[^3] State your number, close your mouth, and let them respond. Filling silence with qualifications or pre-emptive concessions costs you money.

**Action:** Write out your personal three-beat counter script right now — appreciation line, specific dollar target, one quantified achievement as your anchor. Read it aloud until it sounds natural, not rehearsed.

## Lesson 2: Sequence Matters — Base, Then Equity, Then Sign-On

Once you decide to negotiate, the order in which you address each compensation component is as important as the numbers you name.

**Always start with base salary.** Base is a multiplier. Every future raise, every bonus calculated as a percentage of salary, every future job offer that asks for your current comp — all of it is anchored to this number. A $10,000 improvement in base, compounded at typical raise rates, can translate to dramatically more lifetime earnings than a one-time payment of the same size. As one framing puts it, "negotiate signing bonuses — often easier than base salary increases"[^4] — which also tells you exactly why base is harder to move and therefore more valuable when you succeed.

**Second, negotiate equity.** Equity is illiquid and uncertain, which is why companies are often more flexible here than on cash. When negotiating equity, target 30–50% more than the initial grant. Ask for the number of shares or units, confirm the vesting schedule, and understand the strike price or fair market value. "Stock options and bonuses are key parts of the package — it's important to know how they work and their value."[^5] Do not skip this step because the numbers feel abstract; a startup that exits or a public company with strong stock performance will make this the most valuable conversation you had.

**Third and finally, negotiate the sign-on.** The sign-on is one-time money. It does not compound. It does not raise your salary band. But it is often the most flexible lever because it comes from a different budget line and does not set a structural precedent. A common range is 1.5–2x what was initially offered, or a bridge to your target total compensation when base is stuck. The script: *"If there isn't flexibility on base salary, would the company consider a $[X] signing bonus?"*[^6] Frame it as closing a gap, not as demanding extra.

Working through the sequence in this order also protects you from a common trap: accepting a generous sign-on and a below-market base, which feels like a win on day one but costs you compounding dollars every year that follows.

**Action:** For your target offer, write down three numbers — your base target, your equity ask (in shares or units, or a percentage increase on the initial grant), and your sign-on floor. Rank them in the sequence above. Practice transitioning from one to the next if the first item is declined.

## Lesson 3: The Full Board — Negotiating Beyond Compensation

When cash is constrained, the negotiation does not end. Total compensation extends well beyond base salary, and many non-cash elements are negotiable even inside rigid salary bands. "Total compensation extends far beyond base salary, and many elements are negotiable — sometimes more negotiable than salary itself, especially if the organization has rigid salary bands."[^6]

Here is what belongs on your list, roughly in order of long-term career impact:

**Title.** A title upgrade costs the company nothing in cash and can be worth tens of thousands in your next negotiation. "Title upgrade affects future opportunities."[^7] Ask for the title that accurately reflects the scope of the role, not an inflated one — accuracy is the argument.

**Start date.** A delayed start gives you time for transitions, rest, or a competing offer process. It is almost always negotiable and almost never refused.[^8]

**Remote or flexible work.** Remote flexibility saves $5,000–$15,000 per year in commute, childcare, and related costs.[^7] Frame it in productivity terms: "I've done my best work in focused, async environments and I'd like to discuss a 2–3 day remote arrangement."

**PTO.** "PTO is commonly negotiable: 'Given my experience level, would it be possible to start with four weeks instead of three?'"[^6] Asking for five to ten additional days is a standard, non-offensive request.

**Professional development budget.** A $2,000–$10,000 annual learning budget for courses, conferences, and certifications compounds your market value year over year.[^7] Frame it as aligned with the company's interest in a high-performing employee.

**Equipment.** A faster laptop or a home-office stipend for remote roles is a one-time cost for the company and a daily productivity multiplier for you.

When you raise these items, the framing that works is the Total Compensation Approach: *"If there isn't flexibility in the base salary, I'd be interested in exploring other options like [specific alternatives: signing bonus, additional PTO, professional development budget, equity options]. My goal is to reach a total package value of approximately $[amount]."*[^9] This keeps the conversation collaborative and solution-oriented rather than adversarial.

The same principle applies throughout: "approach negotiation as collaborative problem-solving rather than adversarial confrontation."[^6] Employers rarely rescind offers over professional, grounded counter-requests. What risks the relationship is ultimatums and emotion, not data-backed asks.

**Action:** Build your full negotiation list. For each item — title, start date, remote work, PTO, professional development budget, equipment — write one sentence stating what you want and one sentence framing it as a mutual benefit. If you need to prioritize, rank by long-term career impact first, lifestyle value second.

---

## Module Summary

A strong counter follows a simple architecture: appreciate, name one number, anchor to one reason, then stop talking. Sequence your asks from highest-compounding (base) to most flexible (sign-on), and treat non-cash items as a second negotiation table, not an afterthought. Silence after stating your counter is a tactic, not a failure. Every component you successfully negotiate — title, PTO, development budget — compounds across the arc of your career just as surely as the salary does.
