# Module 5 — Contribution Graph & Public Signals

**Objective:** Make the rest of your GitHub presence reinforce pinned work.

---

## Lesson 5.1 — Reading the Contribution Graph Like a Recruiter

The green squares on your profile are not a gamification mechanic. They are a timeline, and recruiters read them that way. A graph that is mostly empty sends a clear message before a single repository is clicked.

The signal recruiters are actually looking for is not daily activity but sustained activity across months. Gaps without any surrounding context read as inactivity — not as vacation, not as a job transition, not as a period of private-repo work. The viewer has no way to know the difference, so they default to the most conservative interpretation.

"Consistent activity — regular commits show dedication (not daily, but weekly)" is the standard articulated by hiring professionals who have reviewed thousands of profiles.[^1] The qualifier matters: not daily. The goal is a rhythm spread over time, not a wall of green from a single obsessive weekend.

What does context look like in practice? A sparse stretch followed by resumed activity on a substantive project is readable. A sparse stretch with no corresponding project, no updated README, no issue activity — that is the gap that costs you. Private contributions do not appear on the public graph by default. If you do meaningful work in private repositories, consider whether it can be made public, or at minimum ensure your public activity reflects something real during the same period.

The reverse failure is just as visible. "A GitHub profile filled with green activity squares might seem impressive but can be misleading. These metrics can be artificially inflated through automated scripts or minor updates to README files."[^2] Recruiters who evaluate technical talent closely know how to recognize padding. A graph that shows 365 consecutive green days alongside three shallow repositories does not add up. It raises a question instead of answering one.

The contribution graph is a supporting document. Its job is to corroborate the quality of your pinned work, not to substitute for it.

**Action:** Open your own profile and look at the last twelve months of your contribution graph. Identify any gaps longer than three weeks. For each gap, ask: does my public activity around that period explain it? If not, plan one substantive public commit per week going forward — not padding, but genuine incremental progress on a real project.

## Lesson 5.2 — Visibility Is a Prerequisite

Recruiters can only evaluate what they can see. This sounds obvious, but the implication is often ignored: work that is private does not exist from a hiring perspective, and a profile cluttered with low-quality public repositories actively undermines the strong work sitting alongside it.

"Prioritize making your best and most complete work public. Keep learning projects or unfinished experiments private."[^3] This is the operating principle. The default tendency is to make everything public because public feels like more. It is not. A recruiter scanning your profile who encounters five abandoned tutorial repositories before finding your flagship project is a recruiter who may not get that far.

The case for decluttering is direct. "Employers prefer 3–5 polished repositories over dozens of unfinished or half-baked experiments."[^1] Moving weak repositories to private is not hiding work — it is curation. The same editorial judgment that goes into a pinned selection should govern what remains visible at all.

There is also a specific failure mode worth naming. "Before submitting your resume with your newly-linked GitHub profile, make sure it's accessible by the hiring team. If your repo is set to private, no one will be able to see all of your hard work."[^4] The inverse applies equally: repositories that are public but that you would be embarrassed to walk through in an interview are liabilities. Check the visibility setting on every repository before you share your profile link.

The practical question is what "best work" means. It is not most recent. It is not most starred. "Pin your best 6 repositories. Not your most recent — your best. Choose projects that demonstrate range: one full-stack app, one library or tool, one open-source contribution."[^5] Visibility decisions should follow the same logic: if a repository does not belong in that shortlist, it probably does not need to be public.

**Action:** Audit your public repositories this week. Make a list of every public repo. Mark each one as either "supports my candidacy" or "neutral or negative." Move every neutral-or-negative repository to private. What remains should be work you would click on yourself.

## Lesson 5.3 — Open-Source Contributions as Collaboration Evidence

Pinned repositories demonstrate what you can build alone. Open-source contributions demonstrate something different and, for many roles, more valuable: the ability to work within someone else's codebase, follow established conventions, communicate with maintainers, and ship code that meets an external standard.

"Contributing to open source is one of the most powerful signals on a self-taught developer's resume. It proves you can read other people's code, follow contribution guidelines, communicate with a team, and ship code that meets someone else's standards."[^5] This is the distinction that solo projects cannot replicate. Your own repository has no reviewer, no merge gate, no maintainer who can reject your PR. An accepted contribution to an external project has all three, and the acceptance itself is a third-party endorsement.

Recruiters who evaluate GitHub profiles with rigor know where to look. "Start by examining accepted pull requests in well-established projects. When a candidate's code is merged into respected repositories, it's a strong indicator of their technical skills. Maintainers of these projects have already reviewed and approved the code, validating that it meets quality standards and effectively addresses real-world issues."[^2] The merge is the signal. A fork with no commits, or a PR that was closed without merging, does not carry the same weight.

Contributions also extend beyond code. "Dive into issue discussions and pull request comments to assess their collaboration and communication skills, as well as their ability to explain technical decisions clearly."[^2] A well-written issue report, a constructive review comment, or a clear response to maintainer feedback is evidence of professional communication — the kind of evidence that a pinned repository built in isolation simply cannot provide.

The barrier to entry is lower than it appears. You do not need to contribute to a major framework. "You do not need to contribute to React or Linux. Start small: fix documentation errors or typos in projects you use. This gets you familiar with the contribution workflow. Look for 'good first issue' labels on GitHub."[^5] Two or three merged contributions to recognized projects, documented clearly on your resume and visible on your profile, carry significant weight relative to the effort required.

One final point on gaming: the same anti-padding logic that applies to the contribution graph applies here. "A steady history of activity suggests reliability and follow-through, while sporadic bursts may indicate less commitment."[^2] A cluster of trivial PRs filed in the week before you send applications is detectable. The pattern that actually impresses is a modest, consistent trail of genuine contributions over time.

**Action:** Find one active open-source project in your target technology stack and identify one "good first issue" or documentation gap. Open a pull request this week. Even if small, a merged contribution from a recognized project is a public signal that no solo repository can replicate.

---

## Module Summary

Your contribution graph and public repository list work together as a background signal that either reinforces or undermines your pinned projects. Consistent, honest activity across months reads as a working developer; gaps and padding both create doubt. Keep only your strongest work public — move clutter to private — so that every repository a recruiter can click reflects your actual standard. Open-source contributions add a dimension that solo projects cannot: third-party validation of your ability to work within a team's codebase, communicate with maintainers, and meet an external quality bar. Build that trail steadily, not in a sprint before job applications go out.
