# Module 3 — Pinned Repos: Six Slots, Pick Carefully

GitHub hands you six pinned slots at the top of your profile. No other feature on the platform concentrates a reviewer's attention quite like this one. Used well, those six cards become the headline act of everything you have built. Used carelessly, they become the fastest way to lose a recruiter's interest before they read a single line of code.

---

## Lesson 1: Understand What Those Six Slots Actually Are

Most developers treat pinning as an afterthought — they click "Customize your pins," grab whatever looks recent, and move on. That is a mistake, because those six positions do not just organise your profile: they *are* your portfolio.

"You can pin up to six repositories to your profile. These are the most important six links on your entire GitHub. Choose them carefully."[^1]

When a hiring manager or recruiter lands on your profile, they are not going to scroll through 40 repositories. Research consistently shows that attention is short. "Hiring managers don't have time to examine each one, and it's likely they'll only spend a few seconds to a minute before they decide if they're going to pursue your candidacy."[^2] The pinned row is the only guaranteed real estate you control. Everything below it is noise to a time-pressed reviewer.

This changes how you should frame the selection decision. You are not curating a complete record of your learning journey. You are programming six seconds of first impression. Think of it the way a retail display manager thinks about eye-level shelving: "Pinned repositories are your storefront window. Most reviewers will look at those first, so choose them like you're choosing what to place at eye level."[^3]

One practical implication: an empty slot is better than a weak pin. Leaving four strong projects pinned beats forcing in two mediocre ones to fill the grid. There is no penalty for pinning fewer than six; there is a very real cost to pinning something that undermines the impression the other cards create.

**Action:** Open your GitHub profile right now. Count your current pins. For each one, ask: "Would I be comfortable walking a senior engineer through this repo in an interview?" If the answer is no, unpin it immediately — even before you have a replacement ready.

---

## Lesson 2: What a Pin-Worthy Project Actually Looks Like

Knowing you need strong projects and knowing how to identify them are two different things. The criteria are more specific than most people assume.

**Visible output.** A project that can be seen is a project that gets remembered. "Include live demos when possible — recruiters want to click and see something work, not just read about it."[^4] A deployed URL or a screenshot in the README transforms an abstract repository into tangible evidence of shipping ability. GitHub's own documentation echoes this: its pre-application checklist includes "At least one pinned repository has a live demo."[^1] If none of your current projects are deployed, prioritise getting one live before anything else in this module.

**Readable code and clear problem statement.** Every pinned repo needs a README that answers three questions within thirty seconds of reading: what problem does this solve, how do I run it, and what technology did you use? "A repository without a README is a red flag. It suggests you do not think about other developers who might need to understand your code."[^1] The description field shown on the pin card itself matters too, because it is visible without clicking. "Write a short description of the project — just what the project does and the technology used... This is important because this information is visible without the person having to click on the repository to know more."[^1]

**Range within relevance.** A strong set of pins proves you can build across layers of a system without looking unfocused. "A strong set often includes one 'big' project that shows end-to-end ability, plus two or three smaller projects that show quality habits like testing, documentation, or UI polish."[^3] If you are targeting full-stack roles, pin both frontend and backend work. If you specialise, use the slots to show depth rather than breadth — but always keep the target role in mind. "Make sure these projects are relevant to the work you want to do — if you want to be a back-end developer, your GitHub shouldn't solely display front-end code."[^5]

**Action:** Score each potential pin against three criteria: (1) does it have a live demo or screenshots? (2) does it have a README with a clear problem statement? (3) is it relevant to the role you are currently targeting? Only projects that pass all three belong in your top six.

---

## Lesson 3: What to Exclude — and Why the Bar Is Higher Than You Think

Knowing what to remove is as important as knowing what to add. Several categories of repository feel harmless to pin but actively damage your signal.

**Tutorial clones with no original work.** This is the most common mistake junior developers make, and it is among the most damaging. "Not todo apps. Not calculator apps. Not clones of tutorials you followed. Projects that solve actual problems, even if those problems are small."[^1] The reason is simple: reviewers have seen these projects thousands of times. "Avoid listing obvious tutorials (todo apps, weather apps). Recruiters have seen thousands of these and they don't differentiate you."[^4] If you started with a tutorial, that is fine — but before pinning it, you need to have extended it with original features, solved a real problem with it, or rebuilt it from scratch with something meaningful added.

**Broken builds and abandoned forks.** "I'm amazed whenever I click on a candidate's GitHub profile and the first several repositories I find are broken piles of code from a student project 3 years ago."[^6] A repository that errors on install, links to a dead demo URL, or carries "WIP" in the README for two years signals the opposite of what you want: that you do not finish things, and that you do not think about how others experience your work. The same applies to forked repositories you never meaningfully modified. Forking to contribute is excellent; forking to have a copy sitting inert on your profile is just noise.

**No-README repos.** Even if the code is good, a repository with no README is essentially invisible to a non-technical recruiter and a red flag to a technical one. "Quality over quantity: 3-5 well-documented, complete projects are better than 20 incomplete ones."[^7] If you have a project you are proud of but it lacks documentation, write the README before pinning — not after.

The ordering of the pins you do keep matters just as much as the selection. "You should also put those in order, with your best works at the top. Consider that most people are going to only look at your first one or two repositories, so make sure those are your best ones."[^6] Your strongest project goes in the top-left position. The second strongest goes next. Treat the grid like a magazine cover: the most important story gets the biggest headline.

**Action:** Go through every repository on your profile — not just current pins — and apply this filter: tutorial clone with no original work, broken build, abandoned fork, or missing README. Archive or delete anything that fails. Then re-sort your pins so the top-left card is the project you are most proud of.

---

## Module Summary

Six pinned slots are not a gallery of everything you have learned — they are a curated argument that you can ship real software. Select only projects with visible output, readable code, and a clear problem statement. Exclude tutorial clones, broken builds, abandoned forks, and repos with no README, because each one erodes the signal the other cards create. Order matters: put your strongest project in the top-left position, because most visitors will never scroll past the first two cards. An empty slot is always preferable to a weak pin. Treat the pinned row as the one piece of your profile you control completely, and invest in it accordingly.
