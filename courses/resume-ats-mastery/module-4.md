# Module 4 — Keywords & Tailoring per Job

**Objective:** Mirror the job description's language so the ATS and the recruiter both see a match.

---

## Lesson 4.1 — Where Keywords Actually Live in a Job Posting

Most candidates skim a job posting and apply. You need to mine it.

A job description is not a paragraph to read once — it is a keyword map. Every section concentrates a different type of term, and ATS systems are fed those exact terms when the job is created. If the recruiter typed "customer support" into the ATS filter, writing "customer assistance" on your resume means you disappear — even if the skills are identical.[^1]

Here are the five zones to scan every time:

- **Required skills** — These are the hard filter. If a term appears here and you have the skill, it must appear on your resume in that exact form.
- **Responsibilities** — Look for action verb + skill combinations. "Manage Salesforce CRM" tells you "Salesforce CRM" is a keyword; the verb tells you how to frame your bullet.[^2]
- **Preferred qualifications** — These are optional for the ATS but useful for ranking. If you have any of these, include them.
- **Tools and systems** — Software, platforms, and technical environments mentioned anywhere in the posting are high-priority ATS targets.[^1]
- **Company mission statement or values** — Available on the company website. Weaving one or two of these terms into your summary signals cultural awareness to the human reviewer.[^1]

If a skill or requirement repeats across multiple postings for the same role type, it is an industry-wide standard — not optional.[^2]

**Action:** Open the last job posting you applied to. Highlight every skill, tool, certification, and technical term in each of the five zones above. Count how many appear on your current resume.

## Lesson 4.2 — The 5-Step Tailor

Tailoring sounds slow. Done systematically, it takes 20–30 minutes per application once you have a strong base resume.

Here is the process:[^1]

**Step 1 — Scan the posting for keywords.**
Copy the full job description into a separate document. Highlight every skill, tool, title, and qualifier that matches something you actually have. Do not highlight things you cannot defend in an interview.

**Step 2 — Divide keywords into two lists: skills vs. experience.**
Skills go in your Skills section. Experience-based terms (how you applied a skill) go into bullet points in your Experience section. Keeping them separate prevents you from putting "led cross-functional teams" in a bullet when the posting uses it as a soft-skill qualifier — it belongs in the summary or skills block.

**Step 3 — Mirror the employer's exact phrasing.**
ATS software scans for exact matches. If the posting says "customer support," use "customer support," not "customer assistance." If it says "React.js," write "React.js" not "ReactJS." The words must match the way the filter was programmed.[^1][^3]

**Step 4 — Place keywords in the right sections.**
Incorporate them across your resume summary, skills section, and work experience bullets — not just in one place. Select 8 to 12 skills that match the posting for your Skills section. Your summary should feature the most relevant skills for the role.[^1]

**Step 5 — Double-check formatting and spelling.**
A misspelled keyword fails the scan. A misformatted document makes the whole exercise pointless. Spell-check before you submit.

One additional rule for tech roles: include both the acronym and the full form on first use — "Amazon Web Services (AWS)," "Continuous Integration/Continuous Deployment (CI/CD)." Some ATS systems search for one form but not the other, so covering both guarantees a match.[^3]

**Action:** Take one real job posting and run all five steps. Time yourself — the second time you do this, it will be faster.

## Lesson 4.3 — Keyword Density, Stuffing, and the Right Count

There is a range that works and two failure modes on either side of it.

**Too few keywords:** Your resume never surfaces in the ATS. Approximately 98.4% of Fortune 500 companies use ATS to screen resumes, and the system ranks applications based on keyword match before a human ever sees them. Missing keywords means missing the filter entirely.[^1]

**Too many keywords (keyword stuffing):** Overloading your resume with repeated terms appears unnatural, and some ATS systems flag it. Recruiters who do see it recognize the tactic immediately and it signals a weak candidate. Indiscriminate keyword stuffing can lead some systems to penalize your resume.[^1]

**The right range:** Most resumes include 10 to 20 targeted keywords matched to the specific job. The optimal keyword density typically falls between 0.5% and 2% — meaning each important keyword should appear approximately once or twice per 100–200 words.[^4]

To hit that range without stuffing, weave keywords into context rather than listing them in isolation. "Managed Salesforce CRM to track 500+ accounts" demonstrates the skill in action. A bullet that reads "Salesforce CRM Salesforce CRM customer support" gets flagged.

Beyond hard skills, do not ignore soft skills entirely. Employers still value terms like "cross-functional collaboration" or "stakeholder communication" — but make them specific. "Led weekly cross-functional syncs with 4 engineering teams" is better than listing "teamwork" as a standalone keyword.[^1]

Finally, only include keywords that accurately reflect your real experience. If a term shows up in an interview question and you have nothing to say, you have burned the relationship with that recruiter. Relevance and honesty are not in conflict with ATS optimization — they are requirements for making it past the interview.[^1][^5]

**Action:** Pick one real job posting, extract 15 keywords from the five zones in Lesson 4.1, and inject them naturally into your summary, skills section, and top experience entry. Count the total words in those three sections and verify your density stays between 0.5% and 2%.

---

## Module Summary

Tailoring is not optional, and it is not enough to be qualified. The ATS ranks by keyword match before any human reviews your application. Each posting is a map: mine the five zones, separate skills from experience, mirror exact phrasing, distribute keywords across all three main sections, and keep density in the 0.5–2% range. A generic resume does not survive contact with a competitive applicant pool.
