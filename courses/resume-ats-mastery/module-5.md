# Module 5 — The Cover Letter That Earns a Read

**Objective:** Add the context your resume can't carry.

Your resume lists what you did. The cover letter explains why it matters here, at this company, for this role. Most cover letters fail not because they're too short or too long, but because they start wrong, talk about the wrong things, and close weakly. This module fixes all three.

---

## Lesson 1 — Structure and Greeting: Set the Stage Before You Say a Word

A cover letter has a fixed skeleton. Don't reinvent it. The standard structure is: professional header, greeting, opening paragraph, body paragraph(s), closing paragraph, and sign-off.[^1]

Work through it in order.

**Header.** Include your full name, phone number, email, and optionally your LinkedIn URL. Put the date below your contact block, then the hiring manager's name, title, company name, and address. Keep the font the same as your resume — Arial, Calibri, or Times New Roman at 10–12pt. Left-align everything.[^2]

**Greeting.** Address the letter to a specific person. "Dear Ms. Johnson" gets more attention than "To Whom It May Concern."[^2] If the job posting doesn't name the hiring manager, check the company's staff page, search LinkedIn, or call the front desk and ask. If you genuinely cannot find a name, "Dear Hiring Manager" is acceptable. What is not acceptable: "Dear Sir or Madam," "Hi," or leaving the greeting blank.[^3]

One more formatting note that matters for ATS: save the final file as a PDF or .docx, left-aligned, single-spaced with a blank line between paragraphs, and name the file `FirstName_LastName_Cover_Letter.pdf`. Recruiters process many files; a clear name saves a small friction that works in your favor.[^3]

**Action:** Find the hiring manager's name for one real job you're targeting. Use LinkedIn, the company's About page, or a quick phone call.

---

## Lesson 2 — The Hook and the Body: Lead with Value, Not Biography

Most cover letters open the same way: "I am writing to express my interest in the [role] position at [company]." That line says nothing. The hiring manager already knows why you are writing — you attached a resume.

Lead with a hook instead. A strong cover letter introduction should mention a relevant achievement or align with the company's mission statement.[^2] Dive into your areas of expertise and align them closely with the company's core business from the very first line. At the end of the opening paragraph, signal how your experience adds value from day one.[^4]

Here is the contrast in plain terms:

- **Weak:** "I am writing to express my interest in the Software Developer role at Tech Innovations Inc."
- **Strong:** "As a developer with five years building production Java and Python systems, I want to bring that same focus on reliability to Tech Innovations' infrastructure work."

The first sentence is filler. The second tells the hiring manager something immediately useful.[^2]

The body paragraph — one or two short paragraphs or a tight bullet list — answers one question: what value will you bring?[^4] Do not restate your resume line by line. Add context and impact. Choose two to three of your most relevant achievements, quantify them where possible, and tie each point directly to the company's needs.

After every sentence you write, ask yourself: "Does this show how I can contribute to this company?" If the answer is no, cut the sentence.[^4]

Mirror keywords from the job description in your body. This matters because many recruiters scan the cover letter the same way the ATS scans the resume. Keep the entire document to one page, three to four short paragraphs.[^3]

**Action:** Write the opening paragraph for one real application. Start with a specific achievement or a direct alignment with the company's work. Do not use the phrase "I am writing to express my interest."

---

## Lesson 3 — The Closing: Ask for What You Want

The closing paragraph is your last impression. Most candidates waste it by trailing off with something vague like "I hope to hear from you." That is passive. Use it to do three things in order: thank the hiring manager briefly, restate why you are the right fit in one sentence, and give a clear call to action.[^1]

The call to action is not optional. It invites the next step. Express your enthusiasm to meet and discuss the value you can bring, then include your contact details at the end so the hiring manager does not have to scroll back to the top.[^4] You can also take the lead: instead of "I look forward to hearing from you," consider "I would welcome the opportunity to discuss how my background in [X] can support [specific company goal]."[^3]

Close with a professional sign-off: "Sincerely," "Best regards," or "Thank you for your consideration." Follow it with your full name. For digital submissions, add your phone and email below the name. Do not use "Yours," "Take care," or "Cheers."[^3]

One structural reminder: the ideal cover letter is 250–400 words on a single page. Recruiters may scan first, but a well-structured, relevant cover letter can influence shortlisting decisions and strengthen your overall application.[^2] Shorter is almost always better than longer.

**Action:** Write a closing paragraph for the same application you drafted in Lesson 2. Include a specific call to action — name the role, name what you want (a conversation, a call, an interview), and end with your contact details.

---

## Module 5 Summary

The cover letter is not a second resume. It is a short argument that answers three questions a hiring manager has before they even read it: Who are you? What can you do for us specifically? Do you want this job or just any job?

Structure sets the stage — header, named greeting, hook, body, call to action, sign-off. The hook in line one makes or breaks the read. The body answers the value question with specifics. The closing asks for the interview.

Do those three things, keep it to one page, and your cover letter will earn a read.
