# Module 10 — Land the Role

**Objective:** Take the judgment you built and the flagship you shipped, and turn them into an offer. Align your GitHub, résumé, and LinkedIn so they tell one coherent story — then walk into the interview able to answer the one question that defines 2026 hiring: *what did the AI do, and what did you judge?*

> **Who this is for.** Everyone at the finish line — students, career-changers, juniors, non-devs aiming at an AI-adjacent role. This module assumes you've done Module 9 (one real, deployed, defensible project). If you haven't, do that first; everything here points back to it.

---

## Lesson 10.1 — Close the Loop: Make Your Three Surfaces Agree

A hiring manager doesn't meet *you* first — they meet your **surfaces**: your résumé, your LinkedIn, and your GitHub. When those three tell the same story, you read as credible. When they contradict each other, you read as noise. Closing that loop is the cheapest credibility you'll ever buy.

**First, get past the filter — and know what it actually is.** Your résumé usually lands in an Applicant Tracking System (ATS) before a human reads it. Ignore the scary folklore about robots auto-rejecting 70% of résumés — the truth is more useful: an ATS "does not automatically screen out resumes in most cases," it "functions as a centralized database where applications are stored," and "it does not make the decision to reject or advance candidates on its own — that's up to humans" *(alma.me)*. So the real risk isn't a robot rejecting you; it's a poorly-formatted or keyword-thin résumé that never surfaces when a human searches the database, or that parses into garbage. The fix follows directly: "tailor your resume to be ATS-friendly while still catching the eye of hiring managers" *(datacamp.com)* — mirror the real keywords from the job posting, use standard fonts and simple formatting, avoid images/graphics that break parsing *(alma.me)*, and list the actual tools you've used. A "Technical Proficiencies" section naming the AI tools you work with is worth including even if you're not a traditional developer *(resumly.ai)*.

**Then, make the three agree.** Your LinkedIn and résumé should be versions of the same truth, not two different people — the workflows for turning a LinkedIn profile straight into a résumé exist precisely because they're meant to match *(text2resume.com)*. And the link that ties it all together: **every surface should point at the GitHub flagship from Module 9.** Résumé lists it with a live link; LinkedIn features it; GitHub profile pins it. One project, one story, three doors into it.

**Don't leave your skills locked in a box.** The trap for quiet builders, captured perfectly: *"If you've ever seen a music box, when it's closed and just sitting on the table, you never actually get to hear the beautiful sound inside… If you want to be seen, then you have to share your work"* *(datacamp.com)*. This is Module 3's "learn in public" and Module 8's "evidence locker," cashed at the job-hunt: posting your project, your write-ups, your reasoning on LinkedIn is how the box opens. Visibility "can increase your visibility and showcase your passion" to hiring managers *(datacamp.com)*. The skill isn't enough; the *signal* has to escape.

## Lesson 10.2 — Projects as Talking Points

In the interview, your flagship stops being a link and becomes a *story you tell*. Most candidates are terrible at this — "they build amazing projects but can't articulate their impact" *(text2resume.com)*. Doing it well is a learnable, decisive edge.

**Lead with impact, not activity.** The single best piece of portfolio advice, from the author of *Ace the Data Science Interview*: *"The key to standing out is to show your project made an impact and show that other people cared… if you don't somehow quantify the impact, then you are lacking impact"* *(datacamp.com)*. "Built a web app" is activity. "Built a habit tracker that 40 classmates actually used, cutting the thing they complained about most" is impact. Numbers, users, before/after — that's what sticks.

**Structure each story so it lands.** A widely-used frame for behavioral and project questions is **STAR** — *Situation* (the problem and context), *Task* (what you set out to do), *Action* (what you actually did — and decided), *Result* (the outcome, quantified). It keeps you from rambling and forces you to end on impact. Notice the arc maps exactly onto the project you already built: Situation = Module 9's "what problem, for whom"; Action = the decisions you made supervising the AI; Result = the deployed, working thing people used.

**You already wrote the script.** Module 9's "document as you go" was for this exact moment: *"Keep a running log: what you tried, what failed, and what you learned. This documentation becomes your project narrative, and it's what judges, teachers, and interviewers actually want to hear about"* *(figma.com)*. The candidate who logged their decisions walks in with the talking points pre-written. The one who didn't is reconstructing from memory under pressure.

**Tailor the emphasis to the role.** The same project can be told different ways: "a backend-focused startup might care about your API design and scalability… a product company might focus on user engagement and interface design" *(text2resume.com)*. Same flagship, different highlight reel. Read the job, then choose which decisions to foreground.

## Lesson 10.3 — The 2026 Interview Question

Here is the question that now sits under every junior technical interview, whether or not anyone says it aloud: **"What did the AI do, and what did you decide?"** The industry has openly redefined what it's hiring for — *"Tomorrow's developer has less value as a typist and more value as an architectural thinker, critical reviewer and AI collaborator. Adjust your hiring, your training and your assessment criteria accordingly"* *(xylos.com)*. It's the same shift Module 1 opened with: the move from a "craftsman mentality to a strategist mentality," competing on problem-solving rather than coding speed *(dev.to)*. The interview is now an audit of your *judgment*, not your typing.

**Mention AI — carefully, and as a multiplier.** The guidance is consistent with Module 8: *"Emphasize AI as a learning tool, not a crutch. Be prepared to demonstrate you can code without it… Saying 'I use AI to understand concepts faster' is very different from 'I use AI to write my code.' The former shows strategic learning and modern tool usage. The latter raises red flags"* *(frontendmentor.io)*. Your honest, confident frame is the portfolio sentence from Module 8: *"I architected the approach, guided the implementation, and validated all outputs"* *(authenticjobs.com)*. Every verb is a judgment verb. That's the whole answer to the 2026 question, in one breath.

**Be ready to prove the judgment is real.** This is where the Module 9 "explain test" gets graded for keeps. Two of the field's critical interview red flags: *"Can't code during technical interviews"* and *"Building projects you can't explain in interviews"* — the fix for both is the same, **rebuild key parts without AI** *(frontendmentor.io)*. The success marker you're aiming for: *"You can build portfolio projects with or without AI. You're slower without it, but not blocked"* *(frontendmentor.io)*. You don't have to be faster than the machine. You have to be able to stand on your own when the machine is taken away.

**Expect the process question, too.** A common scenario prompt is literally *"Describe a time you used an AI tool to improve a process"* *(resumly.ai)*. Your answer is a Module 6 verification story: here's what the AI gave me, here's the bug/security gap/wrong assumption I caught, here's how I verified it before it shipped. That story proves all three pillars at once — judgment (you caught it), ownership (you took responsibility for the output), communication (you can explain it clearly). It is the single most valuable thing you can say in the room.

> **The framing that wins:** you are not applying as someone who competes with AI at producing code — that race is lost and it was always the wrong race. You're applying as the person who *directs and validates* it. The interview is your chance to show you've already been doing the job the role actually is.

## Lesson 10.4 — The Capstone Checklist

Everything in the course converges here. Run this top to bottom before you start applying — it's the whole ten modules as a single readiness audit.

**The proof surface**
- [ ] **Profile:** GitHub profile is clean, has a README/bio, and *pins your flagship.* (Module 8)
- [ ] **Flagship:** one original, deployed, live project that solves a real problem — not a tutorial clone. (Module 9)
- [ ] **Repos show reasoning:** commit messages and PRs say *why*; AI assistance is honestly labeled. (Modules 7, 8)
- [ ] **No red flags:** nothing deployed only to localhost; no exposed secrets / `.env` committed; no abandoned-after-demo repos. (Modules 7.6, 8.2)

**The judgment**
- [ ] **Explain test passed:** you can walk through the flagship and rebuild its core *without AI.* (Modules 8.4, 9.4)
- [ ] **Verification story ready:** one concrete "AI was confidently wrong and I caught it" story. (Module 6)
- [ ] **Honest AI framing rehearsed:** "architected, guided, validated" — out loud, comfortably. (Module 8.3)

**The alignment**
- [ ] **Three surfaces agree:** résumé, LinkedIn, GitHub tell one story and all point at the flagship. (10.1)
- [ ] **ATS-friendly résumé:** real keywords from the posting, clean formatting, tools listed. (10.1)
- [ ] **Impact quantified:** your project's story leads with a number or an outcome, not "built a thing." (10.2)
- [ ] **Visible:** you've shared at least one piece of your work/reasoning publicly. The box is open. (10.1, Module 3)

If every box is checked, you're not "a beginner hoping to get hired." You're a junior who can demonstrably do the 2026 job. That's a different, much stronger position.

---

## Action — The Final Capstone

This is the last action of the course. Two parts, a couple of focused hours:

1. **Run the full alignment check (10.4).** Go down the checklist honestly. Fix what's broken — usually it's a localhost-only deploy, a contradictory LinkedIn, or a flagship you can't fully explain. Don't apply until the surfaces agree and the flagship is defensible.
2. **Rehearse one flagship talking point — out loud.** Using STAR (10.2), tell the story of your Module 9 project in about two minutes, and make sure it explicitly covers the AI-assisted parts: *what the AI did, what you decided, and how you validated it* (10.3). Record yourself or say it to a friend. The first time should not be in the interview.

Do those two things and you've done what this entire course was for: you turned "AI can write code" from a threat into your advantage, and you built the proof that you're the one who can be trusted to drive it.

> **The through-line, completed:** "AI writes the code now; your job is to out-judge it — and GitHub is where you prove you can." Across ten modules you became the judge (Act 1), proved it on a real build and a real profile (Act 2), and now you can walk into any room and show it. The code got cheap. *Your judgment didn't.* That's the edge — go use it.
