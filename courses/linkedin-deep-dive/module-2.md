# Module 2 — The About Section: Keywords Wrapped in Narrative

**Objective:** Convert your largest text block into a keyword-dense, human-readable pitch that attracts inbound opportunities.

---

## Lesson 2.1 — Know Your Canvas: The 2,600-Character Budget

The About section is LinkedIn's largest freeform text field, and most professionals leave it either blank or stuffed with a copied job description. Neither works.

You have a hard ceiling of 2,600 characters[^1]. That sounds like plenty — until you realize the platform truncates your text almost immediately on mobile. On desktop, visitors see roughly 200–300 characters before hitting "See more"; on mobile the visible window is even smaller[^1]. That means your first two or three lines are doing nearly all the persuasion work. Everything after the fold is read only by people who are already interested.

Think of it as a two-stage pitch. Stage one — the visible opener — has one job: make the reader tap "See more." Stage two — everything below the fold — has a different job: convert that curious visitor into an inbound lead, a recruiter DM, or a connection request.

Because the section is also fully keyword-searchable, every character you spend on generic filler ("results-driven professional," "team player") is a character you did not spend on a term someone might actually search for[^2]. The 2,600-character budget is finite. Spend it deliberately.

A practical target is 1,500–2,000 characters — enough to tell a complete story without testing patience[^1]. Shorter risks thin substance; longer risks abandoned reads.

**Action:** Open your current About section. Paste it into a character counter. Note the total, then identify every sentence that contains no searchable skill, tool, technology, or measurable outcome. Those sentences are candidates for cuts or rewrites in the next lesson.

---

## Lesson 2.2 — Weave Keywords Into Narrative, Not a List

The single most common About section mistake is treating it like a skills dump: a paragraph of comma-separated tools that reads like a résumé appendix. This fails on two levels. Recruiters stop reading. And the algorithm, which scans keyword density across the full section, rewards natural integration — not raw repetition[^2].

The research-backed target is **8–12 relevant keywords** woven throughout your summary[^2]. The method is narrative, not enumeration.

Compare these two approaches:

*List version:* "Skills: Python, SQL, dbt, Airflow, Spark, data pipelines, ETL, cloud infrastructure, AWS, stakeholder communication."

*Narrative version:* "I design and maintain production data pipelines using Python, dbt, and Airflow on AWS — translating raw event streams into analytics-ready datasets that non-technical stakeholders can actually use."

The second version contains the same core keywords. But it also explains what you do with them, which is what a hiring manager reading your profile actually wants to understand[^3].

A few structural habits that help:

- **Spread keywords across paragraphs.** Clustering all your tools in one sentence creates an awkward read and dilutes the signal. Each paragraph should naturally introduce one or two terms[^2].
- **Use the terms recruiters search, not internal company jargon.** If your title was "Growth Hacker," your About section should also contain "growth marketing" or "demand generation" — the words someone types into LinkedIn's search bar.
- **Echo your resume summary.** Consistency across documents reinforces the same terms with LinkedIn's index and with any human reading both.
- **Write for mobile readability.** Large blocks of text are hard to read on a phone screen. Keep paragraphs to two or three lines. Use line breaks to create white space[^2].

The algorithm brings people to your profile. A human has to be impressed once they arrive[^2]. Both audiences need to be served simultaneously.

**Action:** Take the list of keywords from your target job descriptions — the terms that appear in three or more postings — and write one sentence per keyword that places it inside a real work context: what you did, with what tool, toward what outcome. You now have raw material for Lesson 2.3.

## Lesson 2.3 — The Four-Block Structure That Converts

Knowing your keywords is necessary but not sufficient. The order in which you present information determines whether someone reads to the end. A proven structure — used across high-performing profiles — organizes the About section into four sequential blocks[^1].

### Block 1: The Hook (aim for ~200 characters)

This is your pre-fold content. It must instantly communicate who you are and create enough curiosity that the reader taps "See more"[^1]. A mission statement, a bold quantified claim, or a single sharp sentence about the problem you solve all work here. Avoid opening with your job title alone — that information is already visible in your headline.

Strong hook pattern: *"I help [specific audience] achieve [specific outcome] — [brief credibility signal]."*

Your primary keyword belongs here. If a recruiter searches for "senior data engineer" and lands on your profile, those words should appear in the first sentence.

### Block 2: Skills and Tools in Context

One or two short paragraphs that explain your background and methodology[^2]. Name the technologies, frameworks, and domain areas you work in — but always inside a sentence that describes what you do with them. This block carries the heaviest keyword load in the section.

Write in first person throughout. "I" statements feel direct and authentic on a social platform; third person creates unnecessary distance and reads as outdated[^1][^3].

### Block 3: Quantified Achievements

Numbers attract attention and add credibility[^2]. Two or three bullet points work well here — each structured as: action verb + tool or context + measurable result. For example: "Reduced pipeline failure rate by 40% after migrating batch jobs from Airflow 1.x to Airflow 2 on GCP."

This block performs double duty: it provides proof for the claims in Block 2, and each bullet naturally embeds additional keywords — tool names, methodologies, and outcome terms that appear in job descriptions[^2].

### Block 4: Call to Action

End with a clear, specific invitation[^1]. Tell the reader exactly what you want them to do next: reach out about contract work, discuss a specific type of role, connect if they're working on a particular problem. A vague "feel free to connect" is not a call to action. A specific one — "If you're scaling a data team and need someone who can own the full pipeline stack, send me a message" — is.

This closing sentence also reinforces your primary keywords one final time, which matters for both the search index and the human reading it.

**Action:** Draft your About section using all four blocks. Aim for 1,500–2,000 characters total[^1]. After drafting, highlight every keyword in the text. If any of your 8–12 target terms is missing or appears only once in a non-descriptive context, revise that block before moving on.

## Module Summary

The About section is LinkedIn's highest-leverage text field — a 2,600-character canvas that the algorithm indexes for keywords and humans read for substance[^2]. Because mobile visitors see only the first two to three lines before the "See more" truncation, your hook must earn the click on its own[^1]. The body of the section should integrate 8–12 target keywords into coherent narrative — not lists — following a four-block structure: hook, skills and tools in context, quantified achievements, and a specific call to action[^1][^2]. Write in first person throughout; it reads as authentic and direct on a professional social platform[^1][^3]. Align the language of your About section with your resume summary so both documents reinforce the same keyword signal to both algorithms and humans.
