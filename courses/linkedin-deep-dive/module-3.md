# Module 3 — Experience, Skills & Endorsements

**Objective:** Make every role entry rank in search and read as proof of competence.

---

## Lesson 3.1 — Write Experience Bullets That Do Double Duty

Your Experience section is where most LinkedIn profiles lose the game. Professionals paste in job descriptions, list duties, and call it done. That approach fails on two fronts: it bores the recruiter scanning your profile and leaves points on the table with the search algorithm, which indexes every word you write.

LinkedIn gives you 2,000 characters per role entry. Use them deliberately. Think of each role as a compressed case study, not a task list. The proven structure is: **action verb + what you did + how you did it + measurable result**. That formula satisfies both audiences — the algorithm gets keyword-rich text, and the human reader gets evidence.

Compare these two bullets for the same role:

- Weak: "Responsible for managing the data pipeline."
- Strong: "Architected an event-driven data pipeline using Apache Kafka and dbt, cutting reporting latency from 4 hours to 11 minutes and saving the analytics team 6 hours of manual work per week."

The strong version contains searchable technical terms (Kafka, dbt, data pipeline), a clear action verb, and a result with numbers. Every word earns its place[^1].

The CAR format (Challenge — Action — Result) is the clearest template for this: describe the situation you inherited, the specific thing you built or changed, and the quantified outcome[^2]. Not every bullet needs all three elements, but your strongest two or three per role should.

A few rules for the experience section:

- Lead every bullet with a past-tense action verb ("Designed," "Reduced," "Launched," "Negotiated").
- Avoid vague openers like "Responsible for" or "Helped with" — they signal passive involvement.
- Include relevant keywords from the job descriptions of roles you want next. LinkedIn scans these descriptions for relevance matching[^3].
- Add media where possible: slide decks, case study PDFs, GitHub links. Tangible proof keeps visitors on your profile longer, which feeds positive signals to the algorithm[^1].

Start with your current or most recent role and work backward. For each role, aim for at least three bullets — enough to establish a pattern of impact without padding[^4].

**Action:** Pick your most recent role. Open the experience editor and rewrite every bullet using the action + what + how + result structure. Count your characters — if you are well under 2,000, you are leaving keyword space unused.

## Lesson 3.2 — Job Titles Are Search Infrastructure

Most professionals treat their job title as a fixed label. On LinkedIn it is a search field, and it carries significant algorithmic weight.

LinkedIn's search algorithm gives high priority to exact keyword matches in your current position title[^3]. One well-placed keyword in your title can outperform the same keyword scattered across your summary. This means "Senior Software Engineer" will surface your profile when a recruiter types exactly that — but an internal title like "IC4 Backend Craftsperson" will not.

If your company uses a non-standard or internal-only title, the fix is straightforward: append the industry-standard equivalent in parentheses. Examples:

- `Pod Lead (Engineering Manager)`
- `Growth Hacker (Digital Marketing Manager)`
- `Staff Wizard (Principal Software Engineer)`

This approach preserves honesty — the real title is there — while ensuring the searchable keyword is present[^3]. Recruiters search for "Engineering Manager," not "Pod Lead."

The same logic applies to seniority indicators. If your title includes "Senior," "Lead," "Staff," or "Principal," keep it. Recruiters filter by seniority level, and those terms matter to the algorithm[^5].

Your title in the Experience section and your Headline work together. The headline (covered in Module 2) is the single most important field, but the experience title reinforces it. If your headline says "Engineering Manager" and your current role title says the same, you reinforce the match signal. Inconsistency between the two dilutes it[^6].

One more consideration: consistency between your LinkedIn profile and your resume. Recruiters routinely cross-reference both[^7]. Titles, dates, and employer names must match exactly. A mismatch — even a minor one like "Jan 2022" on LinkedIn versus "2022" on your resume — raises a red flag in a background check or ATS screen[^8]. Bullet phrasing can differ between the two documents; facts cannot.

**Action:** Open your Experience section and compare every job title against your resume. For any non-standard or internal-only title, add the industry-standard equivalent in parentheses. Verify that all dates and employer names match your resume exactly.

## Lesson 3.3 — Skills and Endorsements: Your Search Tag Cloud

The Skills section is the most underused lever on LinkedIn. Most professionals either leave it sparse or fill it randomly. Done right, it functions as a structured tag cloud that determines whether you appear in filtered recruiter searches.

LinkedIn allows up to 50 skills[^9]. Use every slot. Each skill is a keyword the algorithm can match against recruiter queries. If a recruiter filters for "Python AND AWS AND Docker," your profile only appears if all three are listed[^10]. A missing skill is an invisible wall between you and an inbound opportunity.

The priority order for filling your 50 slots:

1. **Pin your top 3.** LinkedIn lets you select three pinned skills that appear prominently at the top of the section. Choose the three terms most central to your target role — these are what recruiters see before they scroll[^9]. They also carry heavier weighting in the algorithm[^3].
2. **Core role skills next.** The foundational terms for your target function: "Product Management," "Software Engineering," "Data Analysis," "DevOps," etc.[^9].
3. **Specific tools and technologies.** Exact-match tool names matter because recruiters filter on them: "Kubernetes," "Figma," "Salesforce," "dbt," "Terraform"[^10].
4. **Methodologies and frameworks.** "Agile," "Scrum," "JTBD," "MEDDIC," "Six Sigma" — these index for specialized searches[^9].
5. **Soft skills and domain keywords last.** "Leadership," "Cross-functional Collaboration," "FinTech," "B2B SaaS" — these fill remaining slots and broaden your surface area.

Skills with endorsements carry more weight than unendorsed ones in skill-based search ranking[^3]. They also provide social proof — a recruiter reading "Python" endorsed by 14 people reads differently than "Python" endorsed by zero[^11].

Gather endorsements strategically: ask 5–10 colleagues to endorse your top three skills specifically, not generically. "Would you endorse me for Kubernetes and System Design?" produces better results than "Can you endorse me on LinkedIn?"[^10]. Endorse others first — reciprocation is common[^12].

LinkedIn Skill Assessments are worth taking for your strongest technical skills. Passing earns a "Verified" badge that increases your search ranking for that skill[^10].

Finally, keep your skills list targeted. An unfocused list of 50 unrelated skills can signal a scattered professional profile[^3]. Cross-reference job descriptions for the roles you want and ensure the exact terms they use are present in your list[^13].

**Action:** Count your current skills. If you have fewer than 30, add skills until you reach 40–50, prioritizing exact-match keywords from 3–5 target job descriptions. Reorder your list so the three pinned skills are the most strategically important terms for your next role.

---

## Module Summary

The Experience, Skills, and Endorsements sections work as a system. Your role descriptions supply keyword-rich evidence of competence — written with action verbs, measurable results, and the full 2,000-character budget. Your job titles anchor the search match signal; non-standard titles need a parenthetical industry equivalent so recruiters can actually find you. The Skills section extends your searchable surface area to 50 slots, with your pinned three doing the heaviest lifting. Endorsements convert that list from self-reported claims into socially validated signals. Consistency between LinkedIn and your resume ties the whole picture together — any factual mismatch (title, date, employer) breaks trust with both the algorithm and the human reviewer. Treat these three sections as infrastructure, not decoration, and inbound recruiter traffic follows.
