# Module 1 — Headline & Photo: Your Search Result

**Objective:** Make recruiters who search find you, and make the result look click-worthy.

---

## Lesson 1.1 — The Headline Is Your Search Ranking

Your LinkedIn headline is not a job title box. It is the highest-weighted field in LinkedIn's search algorithm[^1]. When a recruiter types "product manager SaaS B2B" into LinkedIn Recruiter, the platform scores every profile against those exact terms — and the profiles with the strongest keyword match in the headline and current title win[^1]. One well-placed keyword in your headline can outperform several keywords scattered across the rest of your profile[^1].

The field allows 220 characters[^2], but on mobile devices only the first 60 to 80 characters are visible before text is cut off[^1]. That invisible truncation line is one of the most consequential pieces of real estate on your entire profile. If your primary role keyword is buried after a string of adjectives and company names, mobile search results will never surface it.

LinkedIn's algorithm works like a mini ATS: recruiters search for specific role labels — "React developer," "data analyst," "senior product manager" — and profiles that do not contain those exact words simply do not appear in results[^3]. Being qualified is not enough if the algorithm cannot match you to the query.

**What this means in practice:** treat the headline as the title tag of a web page. The most important keyword must appear first, not last. Generic descriptors like "hardworking," "passionate," or "results-driven" consume characters without adding search value and should be cut entirely[^1].

**Action:** Open LinkedIn on your phone right now. Read only what is visible in your headline before it truncates. Does your primary role keyword appear in those first 60–80 characters? If not, rewrite it so it does before moving to the next lesson.

---

## Lesson 1.2 — The Formula: Role | Specialty/Stack | Outcome or Target

Because every character in the visible window matters, your headline needs a repeatable structure that places keywords first and strips filler. The formula that consistently surfaces in recruiter searches follows this shape:

**`Role | Specialty / Stack | Outcome or Target`**

A few tech-specific examples in this pattern[^2]:

- `Senior Full-Stack Developer | React, Node.js, AWS | Building Scalable Web Apps for 500K+ Users`
- `Data Engineer | Python, Spark, Snowflake | Turning Raw Data into Revenue Decisions`
- `Engineering Manager | Platform & Infrastructure | Scaling Teams from 5 to 50`

Notice the structure: the searchable role label comes first, specific technologies or specialties appear in the middle segment, and the final segment signals the outcome you produce or the target you are pursuing. The middle segment is where tech professionals often undersell themselves — listing only a job function while omitting the stack or domain that recruiters actually filter by[^3].

**Consistency check — mirror your resume.** Your LinkedIn headline and your resume's role keyword should be identical or near-identical. When a recruiter finds you on LinkedIn and then reads your resume, mismatched titles create friction and reduce trust. If your resume says "Software Engineer" but your LinkedIn headline says "Tech Lead & Innovator," the recruiter's keyword search may never have found the LinkedIn profile in the first place. Use the same primary role label across both documents.

**What to strip.** Avoid: current company name in the headline (it is already shown below), graduation year, buzzwords like "guru" or "ninja," and phrases such as "open to opportunities" (use LinkedIn's built-in Open to Work signal instead). Every word that is not a keyword or a specific outcome is a wasted character[^1].

You have 220 characters — use around 120–160 for a headline that is easy to scan on both desktop and mobile. Aim for three distinct segments separated by pipe characters (`|`) for clarity[^1].

**Action:** Draft your new headline using the `Role | Specialty/Stack | Outcome or Target` formula. Paste it into a character counter and confirm it is under 220 characters. Then compare the role keyword to the title line on your resume — they should match.

## Lesson 1.3 — Photo, Banner, and Name Field: The Click Decision

A recruiter's search returns a list of thumbnail results. Each result shows your headline, your name, your photo, and nothing else. The decision to click your profile happens entirely on that list view. Getting found via keywords is step one; getting clicked is step two, and the photo drives it.

Profiles with a professional photo receive **21 times more views and 36 times more messages** than profiles without one[^4]. A separate data point shows profiles with photos are **14 times more likely to be viewed** than those without[^5]. No amount of headline optimization compensates for a missing or unprofessional photo — the algorithm surfaces you, but the photo controls whether anyone opens the result.

**What makes a photo work:**

- Head and shoulders shot, face taking up roughly 60% of the frame[^5]
- Neutral or lightly blurred background — avoid busy environments[^4]
- Attire appropriate to your industry; for tech roles, smart casual is standard[^4]
- A genuine smile — smiling photos are rated significantly more approachable than closed-mouth expressions[^5]
- Minimum 400×400 pixels; blurry or pixelated images undermine credibility[^4]
- Set the photo visibility to Public so it appears in search results and to people outside your immediate network[^6]

**The banner image signals your domain.** The banner (also called the cover or background image) is the first thing a visitor sees when they land on your full profile page. The default LinkedIn blue communicates nothing[^1]. Treat the banner as a billboard: include your specialization, a tagline, your tech stack, or a graphic that places you immediately in your domain[^1]. You can build one for free in Canva at the correct dimensions (1584 × 396 pixels)[^5]. The banner does not affect search ranking, but it affects whether a recruiter who clicks through stays long enough to read your experience section.

**The name field.** Keep your display name clean and consistent with your resume. If you have a preferred name that differs from your legal name, include both — for example, `Jonathan (Jon) Park` — so that recruiters searching either version can find you. Avoid adding credentials, emojis, or keywords directly into the name field — it reads as spam.

**Action:** Update your photo today if it is more than two years old, uses a group shot, or is set to connections-only visibility. Then open your banner, and if it is the default LinkedIn background, create a simple replacement in Canva that names your role and domain.

---

## Module Summary

- The headline is LinkedIn's highest-weighted search field. Front-load your primary role keyword so it appears in the first 60–80 characters visible on mobile.
- Use the `Role | Specialty/Stack | Outcome or Target` formula. Strip filler. Keep it under 160 characters for clean readability. Mirror the role keyword exactly from your resume.
- A professional photo drives 14–21x more views than no photo. Head-and-shoulders, neutral background, public visibility.
- The banner image signals your domain to anyone who clicks through. Replace the default blue with something specific to your specialization.
- None of these changes require rewriting your work history. They are the fastest, highest-leverage edits on your entire profile.
